\chapter{Algorithm 2: Deflected Subgradient Method}
\label{chap:dsm}

In this chapter, we apply the deflected subgradient method with target level (SGPTL) to the ELM LASSO problem.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Motivation: why the plain subgradient method is inadequate for ELM LASSO}
\label{sec:dsm_motivation}

The ELM LASSO problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dsm_objective}
  f(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2}\norm{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}}_2^2 + \lambda\norm{\mathbf{w}}_1,
\end{equation}
is nonsmooth due to the $\ell_1$ penalty. While the plain subgradient method \cite{plain-subgradient-methods} guarantees convergence for convex nonsmooth problems, two structural limitations make it impractical for this problem:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Zig-zagging and slow practical convergence:}
\\The subgradient update $\mathbf{w}_{i+1} = \mathbf{w}_i - \alpha_i \mathbf{g}_i$ \cite{plain-subgradient-methods} uses only the current subgradient with no memory of previous iterations \cite{fundamental-relationships-sg}. For ELM LASSO, this manifests as severe oscillation in poorly conditioned regions where $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ has large condition number. The theoretical rate is $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$, unavoidable for nonsmooth optimization \cite{frangioni2025nonsmooth,bubeck2015convex}, but practical constants are prohibitive.

\item \textbf{Stringent stepsize requirements:}
\\The stepsize $\alpha_i$ must satisfy DSS \cite{DSS} : $\sum_i \alpha_i = \infty$ and $\sum_i \alpha_i^2 < \infty$ (e.g., $\alpha_i = c/i$). This yields extremely small steps at later iterations, causing the algorithm to stall well before reaching desired accuracy on our problem.
\end{enumerate}

These issues are solved by deflection, which injects memory to reduce oscillations. We adopt this approach for ELM LASSO.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The deflection mechanism}
\label{sec:dsm_deflection}

Deflected subgradient methods \cite{dantonio2009deflected,frangioni2025nonsmooth} replace the raw subgradient with a direction combining current gradient and previous direction memory:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deflected_dir}
  \mathbf{d}_i = \gamma_i\, \mathbf{g}_i + (1 - \gamma_i)\, \mathbf{d}_{i-1}, \quad \mathbf{d}_0 = \mathbf{g}_0,
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_i \in (0,1]$ is the deflection parameter. The update becomes $\mathbf{w}_{i+1} = \mathbf{w}_i - \alpha_i\, \mathbf{d}_i$.

For ELM LASSO, this reduces oscillations by preventing immediate direction reversals due to ill-conditioning. The parameter $\gamma_i$ controls responsiveness: $\gamma_i = 1$ (no memory) recovers plain subgradient, while smaller $\gamma_i$ emphasizes $\mathbf{d}_{i-1}$ for smoother trajectories. We choose $\gamma_i$ optimally to minimize $\norm{\mathbf{d}_i}$, maximizing progress under the Polyak stepsize \cite{polyak-stepsize,deflected-subgradient-method}.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Convergence framework: balancing deflection and stepsize}
\label{sec:dsm_convergence_conditions}

For deflected methods on ELM LASSO, the deflection $\gamma_i$ and stepsize $\alpha_i$ must be carefully coordinated. The theoretical framework is in \cite{dantonio2009deflected,frangioni2025nonsmooth}. We use the stepsize-restricted approach \cite{deflected-subgradient-method}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:sr_step}
\alpha_i = \frac{\beta_i\,(f(\mathbf{w}_i) - f^*)}{\norm{\mathbf{d}_i}^2}, \quad \beta_i \leq \gamma_i.
\end{equation}

The condition $\beta_i \leq \gamma_i$ ensures stronger deflection is balanced by smaller steps, preventing large moves in memory-weighted non-descent directions. Since $f^*$ is unknown for ELM LASSO, we replace it with target level $f_{\mathrm{ref}} - \delta$ (Section~\ref{sec:dsm_target_level}), providing an adaptive estimate throughout the run.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Optimal deflection parameter}
\label{sec:dsm_optimal_gamma}

At each iteration on ELM LASSO, we choose $\gamma_i$ to minimize the deflected direction norm:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma_opt}
\gamma_i \in \argmin_{\gamma \in [0,1]} \norm{\gamma\, \mathbf{g}_i + (1-\gamma)\, \mathbf{d}_{i-1}}^2.
\end{equation}
Since the Polyak stepsize has denominator $\norm{\mathbf{d}_i}^2$, minimizing this maximizes step length and progress. The problem is quadratic in $\gamma$ with closed-form solution \cite{deflected-subgradient-method}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma_star}
\gamma^* = \frac{\norm{\mathbf{d}_{i-1}}^2 - \inner{\mathbf{g}_i}{\mathbf{d}_{i-1}}}{\norm{\mathbf{g}_i - \mathbf{d}_{i-1}}^2}, \quad \gamma_i = \min(1,\max(0,\gamma^*)).
\end{equation}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The target-level mechanism}
\label{sec:dsm_target_level}

The Polyak stepsize \cite{polyak-stepsize} requires knowledge of $f^*$. The target-level mechanism circumvents this by maintaining an adaptive estimate throughout the run.

The fundamental relationship for subgradient methods \cite{fundamental-relationships-sg} is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fundamental}
\norm{\mathbf{w}_{i+1}-\mathbf{w}^*}^2 \leq \norm{\mathbf{w}_i-\mathbf{w}^*}^2 - 2\alpha_i(f(\mathbf{w}_i)-f^*) + (\alpha_i)^2\norm{\mathbf{g}_i}^2.
\end{equation}
The ideal stepsize $\alpha_i^* = (f(\mathbf{w}_i)-f^*)/\norm{\mathbf{g}_i}^2$ \cite{polyak-stepsize} maximizes guaranteed reduction but requires $f^*$.

For ELM LASSO, we maintain estimate $f_{\mathrm{lev}} = f_{\mathrm{ref}} - \delta$ \cite{target-level-mechanism}, where $f_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the best function value found and $\delta > 0$ is expected improvement. When accurate, the algorithm makes good progress. When too optimistic, the Polyak numerator becomes very small, stalling the algorithm. This stalling is detected by measuring accumulated distance $r = \sum \alpha_i \norm{\mathbf{d}_i}$: if $r > R$ (patience threshold) without significant improvement, we reduce $\delta \gets \delta \cdot \rho$ with $\rho < 1$ and retry.

\section{The complete algorithm}
\label{sec:dsm_algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsm} presents the full Deflected Subgradient Method with target level for ELM LASSO.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Deflected Subgradient with Target Level (SGPTL) for ELM LASSO~\cite{dantonio2009deflected,frangioni2025nonsmooth}}
\label{alg:dsm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $f$ (ELM LASSO objective),\; $\mathbf{w}_0$,\; $i_{\max}$,\; $\beta \in (0,2)$,\; $\delta_0 > 0$,\; $R > 0$,\; $\rho \in (0,1)$
\State $r \gets 0$;\; $\delta \gets \delta_0$;\; $f_{\mathrm{ref}} \gets \bar{f} \gets f(\mathbf{w}_0)$;\; $\mathbf{d}_{-1} \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$i = 0, 1, \ldots, i_{\max}$}
\State Compute $\mathbf{g} \in \partial f(\mathbf{w}_i)$ \hfill\emph{(subgradient of LASSO)}
\State Compute $\gamma_i$ via \eqref{eq:gamma_opt} \hfill\emph{(optimal deflection)}
\State $\mathbf{d}_i \gets \gamma_i\, \mathbf{g} + (1-\gamma_i)\, \mathbf{d}_{i-1}$ \hfill\emph{(deflected direction)}
\State $\alpha_i \gets \beta\,(f(\mathbf{w}_i) - (f_{\mathrm{ref}}-\delta))\,/\,\norm{\mathbf{d}_i}^2$ \hfill\emph{(Polyak step)}
\State $\mathbf{w}_{i+1} \gets \mathbf{w}_i - \alpha_i\, \mathbf{d}_i$ \hfill\emph{(update)}
\State $\bar{f} \gets \min(\bar{f},\; f(\mathbf{w}_{i+1}))$ \hfill\emph{(update record)}
\If{$f(\mathbf{w}_{i+1}) \leq f_{\mathrm{ref}} - \delta/2$} \hfill\emph{(improvement)}
\State $f_{\mathrm{ref}} \gets \bar{f}$;\; $r \gets 0$
\ElsIf{$r > R$} \hfill\emph{(stalled)}
\State $\delta \gets \delta\,\rho$;\; $r \gets 0$
\Else
\State $r \gets r + \alpha_i\norm{\mathbf{d}_i}$
\EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{best}}$ (iterate achieving $\bar{f}$)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Key innovation: non-monotone optimization.}
Unlike IRLS, which monitors $f(\mathbf{w}_i)$, SGPTL monitors the record value $\bar{f}_i = \min_{h \leq i} f(\mathbf{w}_h)$. This allows intermediate increases in $f(\mathbf{w}_i)$ while exploiting deflection for faster overall progress. The target-level parameter $\delta$ automatically adapts when progress stalls, restarting convergence without manual stepsize tuning.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Parameter calibration for ELM LASSO}
\label{sec:dsm_parameters}

Algorithm performance on ELM LASSO depends critically on four parameters:

\begin{remark}[Parameter roles]\label{rem:dsm_params}
\begin{itemize}
\item $\delta_0$ (initial gap estimate): Estimates $f(\mathbf{w}_0) - f^*$. Too large produces oversized steps and instability; too small yields insufficient progress. Typical: $\delta_0 = c \cdot f(\mathbf{w}_0)$ with $c \in [0.01, 0.5]$.

\item $\rho \in (0,1)$ (target reduction rate): Controls how quickly $\delta$ is reduced when stalled. $\rho = 0.95$ is slower (more iterations, tighter tracking); $\rho = 0.9$ is more aggressive.

\item $R > 0$ (patience threshold): Distance travelled without improvement before reducing $\delta$. Too small causes premature reductions; too large delays adaptation. For ELM with $M \times H$ data, typical values are $R \sim 10$ to $100$.

\item $\beta \in (0,2)$ (step modulation): Scales the Polyak step. Natural choice: $\beta = 1$. Values in $(1,2)$ are more aggressive (less stable); values in $(0,1)$ are more conservative.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\section{Application to the ELM LASSO problem}
\label{sec:dsm_application}

\subsection{Subgradient computation for ELM LASSO}
\label{sec:dsm_subgrad}

For the ELM LASSO objective, decompose $f = q + h$ where $q(\mathbf{w}) = \norm{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}}_2^2$ (smooth) and $h(\mathbf{w}) = \lambda\norm{\mathbf{w}}_1$ (nonsmooth).

By the sum rule for subdifferentials \cite{subdifferential-sum-rule}:
\[ \partial f(\mathbf{w}) = \nabla q(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda\,\partial\norm{\mathbf{w}}_1, \]
where $\nabla q(\mathbf{w}) = 2\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})$ and the subdifferential of the $\ell_1$ norm is component-wise (Section~\ref{sec:intro_optimality}). A subgradient of $f$ at $\mathbf{w}$ is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dsm_subgrad}
  \tilde{\mathbf{g}} = 2\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}) + \lambda\, \mathbf{s}, \quad s_i \in \partial|w_i|.
\end{equation}
We use the minimum-norm element $s_i = \text{sign}(w_i)$ if $w_i \neq 0$ and $s_i = 0$ if $w_i = 0$, which yields the steepest-descent direction most suitable for nonsmooth optimization.

\subsection{Convergence analysis and hypothesis verification for ELM LASSO}
\label{sec:dsm_convergence}

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of SGPTL \cite{dantonio2009deflected,frangioni2025nonsmooth}]
\label{thm:dsm_convergence}
Let $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ be convex. Let $\{\mathbf{w}_i\}$ be the sequence generated by Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsm} with convergence conditions satisfied (stepsize-restricted with $\beta_i \leq \gamma_i$). Assume subgradients are uniformly bounded: $\norm{\mathbf{g}_i} \leq L$ for all $i$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item The record values $\bar{f}_i = \min_{h \leq i} f(\mathbf{w}_h)$ converge: $\bar{f}_i \to f^*$ as $i \to \infty$.
\item Worst-case complexity is $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$: to achieve $\bar{f}_i - f^* \leq \varepsilon$ requires at most $O(L^2/\varepsilon^2)$ iterations.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Verification of theorem assumptions for ELM LASSO.}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Convexity of } $f(\mathbf{w}) = \norm{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}}_2^2 + \lambda\norm{\mathbf{w}}_1$.

The smooth part $q$ has Hessian $\nabla^2 q = 2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} \succeq 0$ (positive semidefinite), so $q$ is convex. The $\ell_1$ norm is convex. The sum of convex functions is convex \cite{convex-functions-frangioni}, so $f$ is convex. ✓

If $\mathbf{X}$ has full column rank (assumed for ELM), then $\nabla^2 q \succ 0$ is positive definite, making $q$ and $f$ \emph{strongly convex}. This improves the theoretical lower bound from $\Omega(L/\varepsilon^2)$ to $\Omega(L/\varepsilon)$ \cite{strongly-convex-frangioni}, but Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsm} as implemented does not exploit strong convexity, so its worst-case rate remains $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$.

\item \textbf{Bounded subgradients for ELM LASSO.}

The $\ell_1$ component has $\norm{\lambda \mathbf{s}} \leq \lambda\sqrt{H}$ (since each $|s_i| \leq 1$). The smooth component $2\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y})$ grows unboundedly as $\norm{\mathbf{w}} \to \infty$, so $f$ is not globally Lipschitz on $\mathbb{R}^H$.

However, the target-level mechanism prevents arbitrarily large steps: since $f$ is coercive (i.e., $f(\mathbf{w}) \to \infty$ as $\|\mathbf{w}\| \to \infty$) and $\bar{f}_i$ is non-increasing, all iterates lie in the compact sublevel set $\{\mathbf{w} : f(\mathbf{w}) \leq f(\mathbf{w}_0)\}$. By Lipschitz continuity on this compact set \cite{lipschitz-continuity-frangioni}, all subgradients are uniformly bounded by some $L$ depending on the problem data and initial point. ✓

\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Complexity context: why SGPTL for ELM LASSO.}
ELM LASSO is nonsmooth and convex. The intrinsic lower bound is $\Omega(L^2/\varepsilon^2)$ for any first-order method on nonsmooth convex problems \cite{frangioni2025nonsmooth,bubeck2015convex}. Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsm} achieves this bound, making it theoretically optimal. Deflection does not improve worst-case complexity; its benefit is reducing oscillations and improving practical constants in the $O$ notation.

\section{Expected practical behavior on the ELM LASSO problem}
\label{sec:dsm_properties}

Based on the theoretical analysis, Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsm} exhibits the following behavior when applied to ELM LASSO:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Non-monotone convergence (key difference from IRLS).}
The sequence $f(\mathbf{w}_i)$ does \emph{not} decrease monotonically; intermediate increases $f(\mathbf{w}_{i+1}) > f(\mathbf{w}_i)$ occur frequently. Instead, the record value $\bar{f}_i = \min_{h \leq i} f(\mathbf{w}_h)$ converges monotonically to $f^*$. Semilogarithmic plots must display $\log(\bar{f}_i - f^*)$, not $\log(f(\mathbf{w}_i) - f^*)$. The algorithm returns the best iterate found, not the final iterate.

\item \textbf{Sublinear convergence: } $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ rate.
On a semilogarithmic plot, the curve flattens progressively. Unlike IRLS (which typically exhibits linear convergence—a straight line on semilog scale), DSM produces a gradually flattening curve. Doubling precision requires roughly quadrupling iterations. Achieving high accuracy ($\bar{f}_i - f^* < 10^{-6}$) on large ELM problems becomes impractical.

\item \textbf{Low per-iteration cost on large ELM problems.}
Each iteration computes one subgradient (two matrix-vector products with $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times H}$), costing $O(MH)$ operations. This is competitive in total CPU time for large $H$ when moderate accuracy ($\varepsilon \sim 10^{-2}$ to $10^{-4}$) suffices, since IRLS requires $O(H^3)$ Cholesky factorization at each iteration.

\item \textbf{Critical parameter sensitivity on ELM LASSO.}
Performance depends sensitively on $\delta_0$, $\rho$, $R$, and $\beta$ (Remark~\ref{rem:dsm_params}). Poor choices cause stagnation or instability. A preliminary parameter tuning phase (e.g., grid search over $\delta_0$ and $\rho$) is essential before full-scale runs.

\item \textbf{No reliable subgradient-based stopping criterion.}
For smooth methods, $\norm{\nabla f(\mathbf{w})} = 0$ signals optimality. For nonsmooth ELM LASSO, $\mathbf{w}^*$ satisfies $0 \in \partial f(\mathbf{w}^*)$, but other subgradients at $\mathbf{w}^*$ can have arbitrarily large norm. Thus $\norm{\mathbf{g}_i}$ can remain large at optimality. The only practical stopping criterion is to monitor $\bar{f}_i$ and stop after an iteration budget or when $\bar{f}_i$ ceases to improve appreciably over a window of iterations.

\item \textbf{Approximate (not exact) sparsity of solutions.}
IRLS produces solutions where small components are \emph{exactly} zero thanks to the weighted least-squares structure. DSM yields solutions where small components are close to zero but rarely exactly zero. A post-processing thresholding step (e.g., set $w_j = 0$ if $|w_j| < \text{tol}$) may enforce exact sparsity for interpretability or downstream use.

\item \textbf{Effect of regularization parameter } $\lambda$ on convergence.
Larger $\lambda$ increases the $\ell_1$ contribution to the subgradient $\tilde{\mathbf{g}} = 2\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}) + \lambda\mathbf{s}$, amplifying oscillations and slowing convergence. The target-level mechanism adapts automatically, but overall iteration count typically increases with $\lambda$. Higher $\lambda$ also produces sparser solutions, as the $\ell_1$ penalty drives more weights toward zero.

\item \textbf{Deflection effectiveness depends on conditioning.}
Deflection reduces oscillations most dramatically on poorly conditioned problems (large condition number of $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$). For well-conditioned ELM data, the benefit diminishes, and the plain subgradient method ($\gamma_i = 1$) may be competitive. Empirically, deflection is most valuable when dimension $H$ is large relative to sample size $M$.

\end{enumerate}
