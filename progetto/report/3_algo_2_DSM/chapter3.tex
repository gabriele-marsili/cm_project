\chapter{Algorithm 2: Deflected Subgradient Method}
\label{chap:dsm}

In this chapter, we apply the deflected subgradient method with target level (SGPTL) to the ELM LASSO problem. We use the notation $\mathbf{w}_i$ for iterates throughout, consistently with Chapter~1. For the convergence analysis of the plain subgradient method and the diminishing-square-summable (DSS) stepsize rule, we refer to Lesson~5 (Nonsmooth Unconstrained Optimization); the specific contributions of this chapter are the deflection mechanism and the target-level adaptation, which enable practical efficiency without prior knowledge of $f^*$.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Motivation: limitations of the plain subgradient method}
\label{sec:dsm_motivation}

The starting point for this algorithm is the \emph{subgradient method}, the natural extension
of the gradient method to convex nondifferentiable functions. Given
$\mathbf{g}_i \in \partial f(\mathbf{w}_i)$, the update rule mirrors that of the gradient method:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:subgrad_update}
  \mathbf{w}_{i+1} = \mathbf{w}_i - \alpha_i\, \mathbf{g}_i.
\end{equation}
However, this method suffers from two structural limitations.
First, it uses only local information (the subgradient at the current point) and retains no
memory of previous iterations.
The result is a pronounced zig-zag behaviour, where the algorithm oscillates between opposing
directions, leading to very slow convergence.
Second, the stepsize $\alpha_i$ must be chosen with great care. A \emph{constant} stepsize
does not work: the algorithm cycles forever without converging. The stepsize must tend to zero,
but not too fast: the standard requirement is the \emph{Diminishing--Square Summable} (DSS)
condition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dss}
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_i = \infty
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\alpha_i)^2 < \infty,
\end{equation}
satisfied, for example, by $\alpha_i = c/i$.
The DSS rule guarantees convergence, but the practical speed is very poor.
These issues are analogous to the well-known zig-zag of the steepest-descent method on
ill-conditioned smooth functions, which is cured by \emph{conjugate directions} (Heavy Ball,
conjugate gradient). In the nonsmooth setting, the corresponding remedy is \textbf{deflection} \cite{dantonio2009deflected}.


% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The deflection idea}
\label{sec:dsm_deflection}

Deflected subgradient methods replace the raw subgradient $\mathbf{g}_i$ with a
\emph{deflected direction} $\mathbf{d}_i$, defined as a convex combination of the current
subgradient and the previous direction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deflected_dir}
  \mathbf{d}_i = \gamma_i\, \mathbf{g}_i + (1 - \gamma_i)\, \mathbf{d}_{i-1},
  \qquad \mathbf{d}_0 = \mathbf{g}_0,
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_i \in (0,1]$ is the \textbf{deflection parameter}~\cite{dantonio2009deflected,frangioni2025nonsmooth}.
The iterate is then updated as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deflected_step}
  \mathbf{w}_{i+1} = \mathbf{w}_i - \alpha_i\, \mathbf{d}_i.
\end{equation}
This can be seen as a nonsmooth analogue of the conjugate gradient method, where the
deflected direction $\mathbf{d}_i$ plays the role of the conjugate direction. The parameter $\gamma_i \in (0,1]$ controls the trade-off between responsiveness and memory: setting $\gamma_i = 1$ recovers the plain subgradient method, while smaller $\gamma_i$ produces smoother trajectories by emphasizing the previous direction $\mathbf{d}_{i-1}$.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Convergence conditions: balancing deflection and stepsize}
\label{sec:dsm_convergence_conditions}

The joint choice of $\gamma_i$ (deflection) and $\alpha_i$ (stepsize) must be carefully
coordinated: if both are too large the algorithm may diverge. Two complementary frameworks
guarantee convergence.

\paragraph{Approach 1: stepsize-restricted.}
The deflection $\gamma_i$ is chosen freely, and the stepsize is constrained via a
Polyak-type rule:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sr_step}
\alpha_i = \frac{\beta_i\,(f(\mathbf{w}_i) - f^*)}{\norm{\mathbf{d}_i}^2},
  \qquad \beta_i \leq \gamma_i.
\end{equation}
Since $f^*$ is unknown in practice, it is replaced by the target level
$f_{\mathrm{ref}} - \delta$ (see Section~\ref{sec:dsm_target_level}). The condition
$\beta_i \leq \gamma_i$ ensures that the step shrinks as the deflection grows stronger
($\gamma_i$ small). The rationale is that a large deflection injects the ``old'' direction
$\mathbf{d}_{i-1}$ into $\mathbf{d}_i$, which may no longer be a descent direction; a
shorter step compensates for this risk.

\paragraph{Approach 2: deflection-restricted.}
An alternative approach constrains the deflection parameter from below based on the stepsize; this is described in Lesson~5 and is not used in our implementation.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Optimal deflection parameter}
\label{sec:dsm_optimal_gamma}

At each iteration, the deflection parameter can be chosen to minimise the norm of the
deflected direction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma_opt}
\gamma_i \in \argmin_{\gamma \in [0,1]}
  \norm{\gamma\, \mathbf{g}_i + (1-\gamma)\, \mathbf{d}_{i-1}}^2.
\end{equation}
In the Polyak stepsize~\eqref{eq:sr_step} the denominator is $\norm{\mathbf{d}_i}^2$;
minimising it maximises the step length and hence the progress per iteration.
We therefore seek the ``most efficient'' direction among all convex combinations of
$\mathbf{g}_i$ and $\mathbf{d}_{i-1}$.
Problem~\eqref{eq:gamma_opt} is a quadratic in a single variable constrained to $[0,1]$
and admits a closed-form solution.
Define $\varphi(\gamma) = \norm{\gamma\, \mathbf{g} + (1-\gamma)\, \mathbf{d}_{i-1}}^2$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi_expansion}
\varphi(\gamma)
  = \gamma^2\norm{\mathbf{g}}^2
+ 2\gamma(1-\gamma)\inner{\mathbf{g}}{\mathbf{d}_{i-1}}
+ (1-\gamma)^2\norm{\mathbf{d}_{i-1}}^2.
\end{equation}
Setting $\varphi'(\gamma)=0$ gives the unconstrained minimiser
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma_star}
\gamma^*
  = \frac{\norm{\mathbf{d}_{i-1}}^2 - \inner{\mathbf{g}}{\mathbf{d}_{i-1}}}
         {\norm{\mathbf{g} - \mathbf{d}_{i-1}}^2},
\end{equation}
and the constrained solution is
$\gamma_i = \min\!\bigl(1,\,\max(0,\,\gamma^*)\bigr)$.

\section{The target-level mechanism}
\label{sec:dsm_target_level}

The Polyak stepsize in its original form requires the optimal value~$f^*$, which is
precisely the quantity we are trying to compute.
Recall the \textbf{fundamental relationship} for the subgradient method~\cite{frangioni2025nonsmooth}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fundamental}
  \norm{\mathbf{w}_{i+1}-\mathbf{w}^*}^2
\;\leq\;
  \norm{\mathbf{w}_i-\mathbf{w}^*}^2
  \underbrace{- 2\alpha_i(f(\mathbf{w}_i)-f^*)}_{\leq\,0\;\text{(good)}}
+ \underbrace{(\alpha_i)^2\norm{\mathbf{g}_i}^2}_{\geq\,0\;\text{(bad)}}.
\end{equation}
The choice $\alpha_i = (f(\mathbf{w}_i)-f^*)/\norm{\mathbf{g}_i}^2$ maximises the
guaranteed reduction in distance to~$\mathbf{w}^*$ at each step; however, it requires
knowledge of~$f^*$.
The \textbf{target-level} mechanism circumvents this by maintaining an estimate
$f_{\mathrm{lev}} = f_{\mathrm{ref}} - \delta$ of~$f^*$, where:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item $f_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the best function value found so far
        (the \emph{record value});
\item $\delta > 0$ is an expected improvement margin---the ``hope'' of how much further
        we can improve.
\end{itemize}
If the estimate is accurate ($f_{\mathrm{ref}} - \delta \approx f^*$), the algorithm
makes good progress.
If the estimate is too optimistic ($f_{\mathrm{ref}} - \delta < f^*$), the Polyak numerator
$f(\mathbf{w}_i) - f_{\mathrm{lev}}$ becomes very small or negative, producing tiny or
ill-defined steps and stalling the algorithm.
When this is detected---after a prescribed amount of travel without significant
improvement---$\delta$ is reduced (multiplied by $\rho < 1$) and the algorithm retries
with a less ambitious target.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The complete algorithm}
\label{sec:dsm_algorithm}

The full Deflected Subgradient Method with target level is given in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsm}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Deflected Subgradient with Target Level (SGPTL)~\cite{dantonio2009deflected,frangioni2025nonsmooth}}
\label{alg:dsm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $f$,\; $\mathbf{w}_0$,\; $i_{\max}$,\;
$\beta \in (0,2)$,\; $\delta_0 > 0$,\;
$R > 0$,\; $\rho \in (0,1)$
\State $r \gets 0$;\;
$\delta \gets \delta_0$;\;
$f_{\mathrm{ref}} \gets \bar{f} \gets f(\mathbf{w}_0)$;\;
$\mathbf{d}_{-1} \gets \mathbf{0}$
\For{$i = 0, 1, \ldots, i_{\max}$}
\State Compute $\mathbf{g} \in \partial f(\mathbf{w}_i)$
\hfill\emph{(any subgradient)}
\State Compute $\gamma_i$ via \eqref{eq:gamma_opt}
\hfill\emph{(optimal deflection)}
\State $\mathbf{d}_i \gets \gamma_i\, \mathbf{g} + (1-\gamma_i)\, \mathbf{d}_{i-1}$
\hfill\emph{(deflected direction)}
\State $\alpha_i \gets
\beta\,(f(\mathbf{w}_i) - (f_{\mathrm{ref}}-\delta))\,/\,\norm{\mathbf{d}_i}^2$
\hfill\emph{(Polyak with target level)}
\State $\mathbf{w}_{i+1} \gets \mathbf{w}_i - \alpha_i\, \mathbf{d}_i$
\hfill\emph{(step)}
\State $\bar{f} \gets \min(\bar{f},\; f(\mathbf{w}_{i+1}))$
\hfill\emph{(update record \textbf{before} testing improvement)}
\If{$f(\mathbf{w}_{i+1}) \leq f_{\mathrm{ref}} - \delta/2$}
\hfill\emph{(significant improvement)}
\State $f_{\mathrm{ref}} \gets \bar{f}$;\; $r \gets 0$
\ElsIf{$r > R$}
\hfill\emph{(stalled: reduce target)}
\State $\delta \gets \delta\,\rho$;\; $r \gets 0$
\Else
\State $r \gets r + \alpha_i\norm{\mathbf{d}_i}$
\hfill\emph{(accumulate travelled distance)}
\EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{best}}$
       (the iterate achieving $\bar{f}$)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


The algorithm's key innovation is non-monotone optimization: the record value $\bar{f}_i = \min_{h \leq i} f(\mathbf{w}_h)$ (not the current iterate) converges to $f^*$. This allows intermediate increases in $f(\mathbf{w}_i)$ while the algorithm exploits deflection for faster progress. The target-level parameter $\delta$ is automatically reduced when progress stalls (distance travelled exceeds patience threshold $R$) to restart convergence.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Parameters}
\label{sec:dsm_parameters}

\begin{remark}[Parameter roles]\label{rem:dsm_params}
The algorithm involves several parameters whose calibration is critical for performance:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\delta_0$ \emph{(initial gap estimate):}
    an estimate of $f(\mathbf{w}_0) - f^*$.
    If set too large, the target $f_{\mathrm{ref}} - \delta$ is unrealistically low,
    producing oversized steps and instability; if too small, the steps are insufficient
    to make meaningful progress.
    A reasonable choice is $\delta_0 = c\cdot f(\mathbf{w}_0)$ with $c \in [0.01, 0.5]$.
\item $\rho \in (0,1)$ \emph{(target reduction rate):}
    controls how quickly $\delta$ is reduced when progress stalls.
    Typical values are $\rho = 0.95$ (slow, more iterations but tighter target tracking)
    or $\rho = 0.9$ (more aggressive reduction).
\item $R > 0$ \emph{(patience threshold):}
    the distance travelled without improvement before $\delta$ is reduced.
    Too small causes premature reductions (overly conservative target); too large delays
    adaptation.
\item $\beta \in (0,2)$ \emph{(step modulation):}
    scales the Polyak step. The natural choice is $\beta = 1$.
    Values in $(1,2)$ yield more aggressive (but less stable) steps; values in $(0,1)$
    are more conservative.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\section{Application to our problem}
\label{sec:dsm_application}

\subsection{Subgradient computation}
\label{sec:dsm_subgrad}

To apply the SGPTL algorithm to our LASSO problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dsm_objective}
  f(\mathbf{w}) = \norm{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}}_2^2
  + \lambda\norm{\mathbf{w}}_1,
\end{equation}
we need to compute a subgradient $\tilde{\mathbf{g}} \in \partial f(\mathbf{w})$ at each
iteration. Decompose $f = q + h$, where
$q(\mathbf{w}) = \norm{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}}_2^2$ is the smooth part and
$h(\mathbf{w}) = \lambda\norm{\mathbf{w}}_1$ is the nonsmooth part.
Since both are convex, the sum rule for subdifferentials gives
\[
\partial f(\mathbf{w}) = \nabla q(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda\,\partial\norm{\mathbf{w}}_1,
\]
where $\nabla q(\mathbf{w}) = 2\mathbf{X}\T(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})$ exists
everywhere.
The subdifferential of the $\ell_1$ norm is given component-wise
(see Section~\ref{sec:intro_optimality}).
A subgradient of $f$ at $\mathbf{w}$ is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dsm_subgrad}
  \tilde{\mathbf{g}} = 2\mathbf{X}\T(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}) + \lambda\, \mathbf{s},
  \quad s_i \in \partial\abs{w_i},
\end{equation}
where $s_i = \sign(w_i)$ if $w_i \neq 0$ and $s_i = 0$ if $w_i = 0$ (minimum-norm
element of the subdifferential, yielding the steepest-descent direction).
In Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsm} and throughout this chapter, $\mathbf{g}_i$ denotes the
subgradient $\tilde{\mathbf{g}}$ evaluated at the $i$-th iterate~$\mathbf{w}_i$.

\subsection{Convergence analysis}
\label{sec:dsm_convergence}

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of the Deflected Subgradient Method \cite{dantonio2009deflected,frangioni2025nonsmooth}]
\label{thm:dsm_convergence}
Let $f \colon \R^n \to \R$ be convex and let $\{\mathbf{w}_i\}$ be the sequence
generated by Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsm} with the convergence conditions satisfied
(stepsize-restricted with $\beta_i \leq \gamma_i$, or deflection-restricted).
Assume that the subgradients are uniformly bounded: $\norm{\mathbf{g}_i} \leq L$
for all~$i$.
Then the sequence of record values
\[
  \bar{f}_i = \min_{h \leq i} f(\mathbf{w}_h)
\]
converges to the optimal value: $\bar{f}_i \to f^*$ as $i \to \infty$.
The worst-case complexity is $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$: to achieve
$\bar{f}_i - f^* \leq \varepsilon$, at most $O(L^2/\varepsilon^2)$ iterations
are required.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The argument builds on the fundamental relationship~\eqref{eq:fundamental}, adapted to
the deflected case. For the plain subgradient
$\mathbf{w}_{i+1} = \mathbf{w}_i - \alpha_i \mathbf{g}_i$, expanding the squared
distance gives
\begin{align}
  \norm{\mathbf{w}_{i+1}-\mathbf{w}^*}^2
&= \norm{\mathbf{w}_i - \mathbf{w}^*}^2
- 2\alpha_i\inner{\mathbf{g}_i}{\mathbf{w}_i - \mathbf{w}^*}
+ (\alpha_i)^2\norm{\mathbf{g}_i}^2 \notag\\
&\leq \norm{\mathbf{w}_i - \mathbf{w}^*}^2
- 2\alpha_i(f(\mathbf{w}_i) - f^*)
+ (\alpha_i)^2\norm{\mathbf{g}_i}^2,
\label{eq:fund_rel_proof}
\end{align}
where the inequality uses the subgradient inequality
$f(\mathbf{w}^*) \geq f(\mathbf{w}_i) + \inner{\mathbf{g}_i}{\mathbf{w}^* - \mathbf{w}_i}$.
In the deflected case, $\mathbf{d}_i$ replaces $\mathbf{g}_i$ in~\eqref{eq:fund_rel_proof}.
The convergence conditions ensure that an analogous relationship holds for the deflected
direction. Under the Polyak stepsize with $\beta_i \leq \gamma_i$:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item The record value $\bar{f}_i$ is non-increasing by construction.
\item Telescoping~\eqref{eq:fund_rel_proof} over $i = 1, \ldots, k$ and using
    $\norm{\mathbf{x}^{k+1}-\mathbf{w}^*}^2 \geq 0$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convergence_rate}
      \bar{f}^{\,k} - f^*
\;\leq\; \frac{L\,\norm{\mathbf{x}^1 - \mathbf{w}^*}}{\sqrt{k}},
\end{equation}
    which gives $k \geq L^2\norm{\mathbf{x}^1-\mathbf{w}^*}^2/\varepsilon^2 =
    O(1/\varepsilon^2)$.
\qedhere
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Key observations on convergence]\label{rem:dsm_convergence_obs}
\mbox{}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Non-monotonicity.}
    It is the sequence of record values $\bar{f}_i$ that converges, \emph{not} the
    sequence of iterates $\mathbf{w}_i$ or function values $f(\mathbf{w}_i)$.
    The method is intrinsically non-monotone: $f(\mathbf{w}_{i+1}) > f(\mathbf{w}_i)$
    can and does happen frequently. For this reason the algorithm returns the best iterate
    found, not the last one.
\item \textbf{Optimality of the rate.}
    The $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ complexity is identical to that of the plain subgradient
    method. This is an intrinsic limitation of nonsmooth optimisation: for convex,
    $L$-Lipschitz functions that are not~$C^1$, the lower bound is
    $\Omega(L^2/\varepsilon^2)$~\cite{frangioni2025nonsmooth,bubeck2015convex}, so the method is worst-case optimal.
    Deflection does not improve the worst-case complexity, but significantly improves
    practical behaviour by reducing oscillations.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Verification of hypotheses for our problem.}
We check that the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:dsm_convergence} are satisfied
by~\eqref{eq:dsm_objective}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Convexity.}
$f(\mathbf{w}) = \norm{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}}_2^2 + \lambda\norm{\mathbf{w}}_1$
    is convex, being the sum of a convex smooth function ($q$, with Hessian
    $2\mathbf{X}\T \mathbf{X} \succeq 0$) and a convex norm.
    If $\mathbf{X}$ has full column rank, $q$ is $\tau$-strongly convex with
    $\tau = 2\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{X}\T \mathbf{X}) > 0$, so $f$ is also strongly convex
    and the minimum is unique.
    In this case the theoretical lower bound for nonsmooth optimisation improves from
    $\Omega(1/\varepsilon^2)$ to $\Omega(1/\varepsilon)$.
    However, the DSM as implemented does not exploit strong convexity, so its worst-case
    rate remains~$O(1/\varepsilon^2)$.
\item \textbf{Bounded subgradients.}
    The $\ell_1$ component $\lambda \mathbf{s}$ has norm at most $\lambda\sqrt{H}$
    (since $|s_i| \leq 1$).
    The smooth component $2\mathbf{X}\T(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y})$ grows linearly
    with $\norm{\mathbf{w}}$, so $f$ is not globally Lipschitz on~$\R^H$.
    However, the target-level mechanism prevents arbitrarily large steps, so the iterates
    remain confined to a bounded set and $\norm{\mathbf{g}_i}$ is uniformly bounded along
    the generated sequence.    
    More precisely, since $f$ is coercive (i.e., $f(\mathbf{w}) \to \infty$ as $\|\mathbf{w}\| \to \infty$) and the record value $\bar{f}_i$ is non-increasing, all iterates lie in the sublevel set $\{\mathbf{w} : f(\mathbf{w}) \leq f(\mathbf{w}_0)\}$, which is compact.
    Lipschitz continuity of $f$ on this compact set then gives a uniform bound $L$ on all subgradients.
  
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Complexity context.}
To understand why DSM is necessary for the nonsmooth LASSO problem, consider the complexity gap between smooth and nonsmooth convex optimization:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Regularity} & \textbf{Convexity} & \textbf{Complexity} \\
\midrule
$f \in C^1$, $L$-smooth     & $\tau$-strongly convex & $O(\log(1/\varepsilon))$ \\
$f \notin C^1$, $L$-Lipschitz & $\tau$-strongly convex & $\Omega(L^2/\varepsilon)$ \\
$f \in C^1$, $L$-smooth     & convex                 & $O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})$ \\
$f \notin C^1$, $L$-Lipschitz & convex                 & $\Omega(L/\varepsilon^2)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Our LASSO problem is in the bottom-right quadrant (nonsmooth, convex): DSM realizes the optimal $\Omega(L/\varepsilon^2)$ rate, which is dramatically slower than smooth methods but theoretically unavoidable.

\subsection{Expected properties on the ELM problem}
\label{sec:dsm_properties}

Based on the theoretical analysis, the following behaviour is expected when applying DSM
to the ELM LASSO problem~\eqref{eq:dsm_objective}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-monotone convergence.}
    The function value $f(\mathbf{w}_i)$ oscillates throughout the run. The quantity to
    monitor is the record value $\bar{f}_i$, which decreases monotonically by
    construction. In convergence plots it is essential to display $\bar{f}_i$ rather
    than $f(\mathbf{w}_i)$.
    \item \textbf{Sublinear convergence.}
    In a semilogarithmic plot ($\log(\bar{f}_i-f^*)$ vs.\ iterations), the curve
    flattens progressively: initial improvements are rapid, but progress slows at each
    decade of precision. This contrasts with the typically linear convergence of IRLS,
    which produces a straight line on the same scale. The reason is the $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$
    complexity: doubling the desired precision requires roughly quadrupling the number of
    iterations.
    \item \textbf{Difficulty at high accuracy.}
    Achieving gaps $\bar{f}_i - f^* < 10^{-4}$ requires a prohibitive number of
    iterations due to the $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ rate. This is a theoretical limitation of
    the approach, not an implementation defect.
    \item \textbf{Low per-iteration cost.}
    Each iteration costs $O(MH)$, dominated by two matrix-vector products with
    $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times H}$. This makes the method competitive in CPU
    time when low accuracy suffices, particularly for large~$H$ where the $O(H^3)$ cost
    of IRLS's Cholesky factorisation becomes prohibitive.
    \item \textbf{Parameter sensitivity.}
    Performance depends critically on the calibration of $\delta_0$, $\rho$, $R$, and
    $\beta$. Poor choices may lead to extremely slow convergence or instability. A
    preliminary tuning phase is recommended.
    \item \textbf{No reliable stopping criterion.}
    Unlike smooth methods where $\norm{\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^*)} = 0$ provides a natural
    stopping criterion, in the nonsmooth case $0 \in \partial f(\mathbf{w}^*)$ but other
    elements of $\partial f(\mathbf{w}^*)$ can have large norm, so
    $\norm{\mathbf{g}_i}$ can remain large even at the optimum. The only practical
    criterion is to monitor $\bar{f}_i$ and stop after a prescribed iteration budget
    or when $\bar{f}_i$ ceases to improve appreciably.
    \item \textbf{Approximate sparsity.}
    Unlike IRLS, which tends to produce components that are exactly zero thanks to the
    weighted least-squares structure, the DSM yields solutions where ``small'' components
    are close to zero but rarely exactly so. A post-processing thresholding step may be
    needed to enforce exact sparsity below a chosen tolerance.
    \item \textbf{Effect of $\lambda$.}
    Larger $\lambda$ produces a larger subgradient component from the $\ell_1$ term,
    which increases oscillations and may slow convergence. However, the target-level
    mechanism adapts to this automatically. The sparsity of the solution increases with
    $\lambda$, as the $\ell_1$ penalty pushes more weights toward zero.
\end{itemize}
