\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}

We studied the ELM~LASSO problem of
Section~\ref{sec:regularization} with two algorithms at opposite ends
of the nonsmooth-optimisation spectrum: IRLS
(Chapter~\ref{chap:irls}), which reformulates the $\ell_{1}$ penalty as
a sequence of smooth quadratic surrogates and inherits the MM linear
rate of Theorem~\ref{thm:irls_convergence}, and SGPTL
(Chapter~\ref{chap:dsm}), which keeps the non-smoothness explicit and
pays the $\Omega(L^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})$ first-order lower bound of
Theorem~\ref{thm:dsm_convergence}. Chapter~\ref{chap:comparison}
combined the two rates with the per-iteration cost analysis
(Table~\ref{tab:comp_cost}) to predict IRLS dominance whenever a
moderate-to-high accuracy is wanted in the $M\gg H$ regime, and the
experiments of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} test that prediction.

\paragraph{Experimental confirmation.}
The predictions of Chapter~\ref{chap:comparison} are reproduced on
every axis tested: linear vs sublinear rate on the synthetic
convergence instance (Section~\ref{sec:convergence}); fixed-accuracy
cost separation of orders of magnitude on the ELM-transformed real
datasets (Section~\ref{sec:real_data}); per-decade iteration cost
constant for IRLS and $\sim\!100\times$ for SGPTL
(Section~\ref{sec:iters-to-eps}). Clarabel converges in the fewest
iterations but factorises an $H\!\times\!H$ system at each one and
ends $\sim\!340\times$ slower than IRLS on \texttt{california}.
Sklearn's coordinate descent does not reach $10^{-6}$ on either ELM
matrix within its own tolerance budget and is not a usable oracle in
this regime. SGPTL closes the gap to $f^{*}$ at large budgets on the
real data (Section~\ref{sec:dsm_long_run}), as
Theorem~\ref{thm:dsm_convergence} predicts.

\paragraph{Recommendation for the ELM LASSO problem.}
In the intended regime ($M\gg H$, fixed $\mathbf{W}_{1}$, output layer
trained offline), IRLS is the recommended solver on the basis of:
faster wall-clock at every tested $H\le 2000$
(Section~\ref{sec:scalability}); fastest to $10^{-6}$ on both real
datasets with agreement to Clarabel within ten significant digits
(Section~\ref{sec:real_data}); hard sparsity at $\varepsilon_\text{thr}$
with no thresholding post-step (Section~\ref{sec:quality}). SGPTL would
become competitive only when $H^{2}\gg M$ or when
$\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}$ does not fit in memory, regimes that do
not appear at the hidden-layer sizes tested.

\paragraph{Limitations and possible extensions.}
The evidence base is two ELM-transformed real datasets and one
synthetic family, all with $H\le 2000$ and
$\kappa(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})$ up to $\sim 10^{6}$
(Section~\ref{sec:datasets}). We did not test designs substantially
more ill-conditioned, nor $H$ large enough that the $O(H^{3})$ IRLS
factorisation dominates while the cheaper $O(MH)$ SGPTL iteration
becomes competitive. The warm-vs-cold SGPTL contrast on
\texttt{california} is partly a statement about the data
(Section~\ref{sec:warm_cold}). A stochastic or block-coordinate
version of SGPTL would lower the $O(MH)$ iteration cost across
mini-batches or coordinate blocks; this targets the
$H^{2}\!\gg\!M$ or out-of-core regimes where IRLS' factorisation is
the bottleneck. On the IRLS side, the inner Cholesky solve can be
replaced by an inexact iterative solver with a tolerance tied to the
outer MM progress, lowering the $O(H^{3})$ factor at large $H$ while
preserving the linear outer rate of
Theorem~\ref{thm:irls_convergence}.
