\chapter{Algorithm Comparison}
\label{chap:comparison}

Both IRLS (Chapter~\ref{chap:irls}) and the Deflected Subgradient Method
(Chapter~\ref{chap:dsm}) solve the same LASSO problem
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^H} f(\mathbf{w})
    = \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 + \lambda_{\text{LASSO}}\|\mathbf{w}\|_1,
    \label{eq:comp_objective}
\end{equation}
but handle the non-smoothness of the $\ell_1$ penalty in opposite ways.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Core strategy: how each algorithm handles non-smoothness}
\label{sec:comp_strategy}

\paragraph{IRLS — smoothing via majorization.}\mbox{} \\
IRLS replaces $f$ with a smooth quadratic majorant $Q(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_k)$
tight at $\mathbf{w}_k$ (Section~\ref{sec:irls_mm}), reducing each step to a
weighted ridge problem solved in closed form via the normal equations
(Section~\ref{sec:irls_subproblem}). The $\ell_1$ penalty is absorbed into
diagonal weights $(\mathbf{W}_k)_{ii}$ that blow up where $|w_{k,i}|$ is small,
driving those components to zero (Section~\ref{sec:irls_idea}).

\paragraph{DSM — direct subgradient with memory.}\mbox{} \\
DSM descends along a subgradient $\mathbf{g} \in \partial f(\mathbf{w}_i)$
(Section~\ref{sec:dsm_subgrad}), deflected with the previous direction
(Section~\ref{sec:dsm_deflection}) to damp zig-zag, with a Polyak step using
a self-adapting target estimate of $f^*$ (Section~\ref{sec:dsm_target_level}).

IRLS solves a sequence of smooth problems \emph{exactly} and is monotone in $f$;
DSM operates on $f$ directly, accepts $f(\mathbf{w}_i)$ may increase, and tracks
progress via the record value $\bar{f}^{\,i}$.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Convergence behaviour}
\label{sec:comp_convergence}

\subsection{Monotonicity}

MM guarantees IRLS is \textbf{monotone}: $f(\mathbf{w}_{k+1}) \leq f(\mathbf{w}_k)$
(Section~\ref{sec:irls_mm}); a non-decreasing plot signals a bug, and an
iterate-change criterion reliably stops the algorithm. DSM is
\textbf{non-monotone}: only the record $\bar{f}^{\,i} = \min_{h \leq i} f(\mathbf{w}_h)$
is monotone, so DSM returns $\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{best}}$ and stops on a fixed budget.

\subsection{Rate}

IRLS achieves a \textbf{linear} rate (Section~\ref{sec:irls_convergence}); DSM
achieves the optimal \textbf{sublinear} $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ rate for
nonsmooth convex functions (Theorem~\ref{thm:dsm_convergence}). Tightening
$\varepsilon$ by one decade costs $\sim 100\times$ more DSM iterations versus
a constant for IRLS.

\subsection{Convergence on the ELM problem}

For the ELM problem $\mathbf{X} = \sigma(\mathbf{X}_{\text{origin}}\mathbf{W}_1^T)$ has full column rank (standard ELM condition when $M \ge H$ with random $\mathbf{W}_1$ and nonlinear $\sigma$), so $f$ is $\mu$-strongly convex with unique minimizer $\mathbf{w}^*$. IRLS iterates $\mathbf{w}_k \to \mathbf{w}^*$ at a linear rate governed by the weighted normal-equation conditioning (Theorem~\ref{thm:irls_convergence}); Theorem~\ref{thm:dsm_convergence} only guarantees $\bar{f}_i \to f^*$, with iterates that may oscillate. Strong convexity would allow $O(L^2/(\mu\varepsilon))$ \cite[Slide 12]{frangioni-slides-smooth}, but SGPTL does not exploit it (Section~\ref{sec:dsm_convergence}).

\section{Computational cost}
\label{sec:comp_cost}

\subsection{Per-iteration cost}

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Operation} & \textbf{IRLS} & \textbf{DSM} \\
\midrule
Weight / direction update & $O(H)$ & $O(H)$ \\
Matrix-vector product(s) & --- & $O(MH)$ \\
Linear system solve & $O(H^3)$ (Cholesky) or $O(pH^2)$ (CG) & --- \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total per iteration} & $O(H^3)$ & $O(MH)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Per-iteration computational cost of IRLS and DSM.}
\label{tab:comp_cost}
\end{table}

IRLS solves an $H \times H$ SPD system per iteration
(Section~\ref{sec:irls_subproblem}): $O(H^3/3)$ via Cholesky, or $O(pH^2)$
via $p$ CG steps when $H$ is large and $\mathbf{Q}_k$ well-conditioned.
The matrices $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$
are precomputed once at $O(MH^2)$ and $O(MH)$.

DSM uses two matrix-vector products per iteration ($\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}_i$
and $\mathbf{X}^T(\cdot)$, each $O(MH)$), no precomputation
(Section~\ref{sec:dsm_subgrad}).

\subsection{Total cost}

With iteration counts $k_{\mathrm{IRLS}}$, $k_{\mathrm{DSM}}$:
\begin{align*}
    \text{IRLS total:} &\quad O(MH^2) + k_{\mathrm{IRLS}} \cdot O(H^3), \\
    \text{DSM total:}  &\quad k_{\mathrm{DSM}} \cdot O(MH).
\end{align*}
Linear vs sublinear gives $k_{\mathrm{IRLS}} \ll k_{\mathrm{DSM}}$, so DSM is
competitive only when $H^2 \gg M$ and the Cholesky cost dominates.

On the ELM problem $M \gg H$ is typical, so $O(MH^2)$ precomputation dominates
IRLS's total. Total costs become $O(MH^2)$ vs $O(k_{\mathrm{DSM}} \cdot MH)$,
and IRLS wins whenever $k_{\mathrm{DSM}} \gtrsim H$. Since
Theorem~\ref{thm:dsm_convergence} forces $k_{\mathrm{DSM}} = O(L^2/\varepsilon^2)$
with $L$ growing in $H$ (the $\ell_1$ subgradient has norm $\le \lambda_{\text{LASSO}}\sqrt{H}$),
this inequality holds at every $H$ we test for any reasonable accuracy.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Solution quality}
\label{sec:comp_quality}

\subsection{Sparsity}

IRLS yields numerically sparse iterates as a byproduct of its weighted
least-squares structure: small components accumulate large weights and are
driven to exactly zero (within $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}}$) at convergence,
no post-processing (Section~\ref{sec:irls_expected}). DSM gives only
\textbf{approximate sparsity}; an explicit thresholding step is needed
(Section~\ref{sec:dsm_properties}).

\subsection{Accuracy}

At equal budget IRLS reaches much higher accuracy on small-to-moderate
problems thanks to its linear rate. DSM is competitive only when
$H^2 \gg M$ (Table~\ref{tab:comp_cost}).

\label{sec:comp_summary}

Preferred when & High accuracy, $H \ll M$ & Large $H$, moderate accuracy \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Summary comparison of IRLS and DSM on the ELM LASSO problem.}
\label{tab:comp_summary}
\end{table}

On the project's regime ($M \gg H$, $\mathbf{W}_1$ fixed, offline training) IRLS is preferred: the $O(MH^2)$ precomputation of $\mathbf{A}$ amortizes over a few cheap Cholesky solves, sparsity comes for free, the single knob is $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}}$, and monotone $f$ doubles as a correctness check. DSM is the better choice only when $H^2 \gg M$ or $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ does not fit in memory, and the accuracy target is moderate ($\varepsilon \sim 10^{-3}$).
