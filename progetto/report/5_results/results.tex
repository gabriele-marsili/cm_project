\chapter{Experimental Results}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter tests the IRLS and SGPTL implementations against the
predictions of Chapters~\ref{chap:irls}--\ref{chap:dsm} (convergence
behaviour on ELM~LASSO) and against the cost--accuracy comparison of
Chapter~\ref{chap:comparison}.

% =====================================================================
\section{Experimental setup and datasets}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Implementation.}
The code under \texttt{progetto/code/src/} is split into five modules:
\texttt{lasso\_utils.py} (objective, smooth-part gradient, subgradient
selection), \texttt{linear\_}\allowbreak\texttt{solvers.py} (Cholesky via
\texttt{scipy.linalg.cho\_factor/}\allowbreak\texttt{cho\_solve} and a
hand-rolled Jacobi-preconditioned CG), \texttt{irls.py}
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:irls}),
\texttt{deflected\_}\allowbreak\texttt{subgradient.py}
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsm}), and \texttt{elm.py} (random hidden layer).
Core algorithms are written from scratch (reusing only NumPy/SciPy as
primitives). A pytest suite cross-checks both algorithms against
\texttt{sklearn.linear\_}\allowbreak\texttt{model.Lasso} and verifies the
surrogate-descent and record-monotonicity invariants iterate by iterate.

\paragraph{Reference solution.}
We obtain $\mathbf{w}^{*}$ from
\texttt{sklearn.linear\_}\allowbreak\texttt{model.Lasso} (coordinate
descent, \texttt{tol}$=10^{-12}$, $10^{5}$ iterations,
\texttt{fit\_intercept=False}). Sklearn's loss has a $1/(2M)$ prefactor on the squared residual and
weights the $\ell_{1}$ term by $\alpha$, so the equivalent
of~\eqref{eq:original_problem} is obtained by passing
$\alpha_\text{sklearn} = \lambda_\text{LASSO}/M$; we then evaluate
$f^{*} = f(\mathbf{w}^{*})$ from~\eqref{eq:original_problem} directly. The returned
$\mathbf{w}^{*}$ satisfies the optimality
condition~\eqref{eq:optimality} below $10^{-3}$ on every instance. On the
ELM-transformed real data, where coordinate descent does not converge in
a realistic budget, we replace this reference with the IRLS-converged
value cross-validated against CVXPY's interior-point solver Clarabel
(Section~\ref{sec:real_data}).

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}
We use three instances spanning two conditioning regimes.

\emph{Synthetic LASSO benchmark} (\texttt{src/data\_generation.py}). We
sample a sparse $\mathbf{w}_\text{true}\in\mathbb{R}^{H}$
($10$--$15\%$ non-zero, entries $\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$), draw
$\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times H}$ with i.i.d.\ Gaussian columns
normalised to unit $\ell_{2}$ norm, and set
$\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}_\text{true} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$,
$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0, 0.05^{2}\mathbf{I})$. This is the benign regime for LASSO. We pick $M\ge 2H$ to keep
$\kappa(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})$ at a moderate constant by
random-matrix concentration~\cite{hastie2009elements}, the planted
$\mathbf{w}_\text{true}$ provides the ground truth for support-recovery
metrics, and the noise std $0.05$ puts the SNR in the moderate-noise
regime (about $5$--$10\%$ of the signal magnitude). It validates the
algorithms in a well-conditioned setting but does \emph{not} reproduce the
conditioning of ELM data.

\emph{ELM-transformed real datasets} (\texttt{diabetes},
\texttt{california}). We load \texttt{diabetes} ($n=442$, $d=10$)
\cite{efron2004least} and \texttt{california\_housing} ($n=20640$,
$d=8$) \cite{pace1997sparse} from scikit-learn, split $80/20$,
standardise features and target on the train split, and apply the ELM
map $\mathbf{X}_\text{hidden}=\sigma(\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{W}_{1}^{\top})$
with $H$ sigmoid units and a fixed random $\mathbf{W}_{1}$. We use
$H=200$ for the main experiments and $H=50$ where an independent
solver (\texttt{cvxpy.CLARABEL}) is affordable for cross-validation. The
sigmoid breaks the unit-norm column property and inflates
$\kappa(\mathbf{X}_\text{hidden}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_\text{hidden})$ to
$\sim 10^{6}$ on both datasets --- orders of magnitude worse than the
synthetic design, which is the stress regime we want to probe.

\emph{Limitations.} (i)~The synthetic benchmark lives in the
well-conditioned, incoherent regime by construction, so positive results
there do not extrapolate to ill-conditioned designs. (ii)~The real
datasets have no ground-truth support, so the
\emph{support-recovery} metrics of Section~\ref{sec:quality}
(precision/recall/F$_1$ against the planted support) apply only to the
synthetic instance; sparsity counts at a fixed threshold remain
well-defined and are reported on real data alongside training-objective
gaps and test MSE. (iii)~On
\texttt{california} at $\lambda_\text{LASSO}=0.1$ the OLS warm start
already sits very close to $f^{*}$ (Section~\ref{sec:warm_cold}); we
account for this in the warm-vs-cold and real-data comparisons.

\section{Convergence and agreement with theory}
\label{sec:convergence}

\paragraph{Setting.}
Synthetic convergence instance, $H=100$, $M=300$,
$\lambda_\text{LASSO}=0.1$, sparsity $10\%$. Both algorithms are run from
the same OLS warm start
$\mathbf{w}_{0}=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}$
so that any difference in the curves is attributable to the algorithm and
not to the initialisation; the one-time Cholesky cost of forming
$\mathbf{w}_{0}$ (Section~\ref{sec:dsm_parameters}) is charged to both
wall-clock totals. The relevant objective values of this instance are
$f(\mathbf{0})\approx 76$ at the origin, $f(\mathbf{w}_{0})\approx 1.56$
at the warm start, and $f^{*}\approx 1.13$ at the sklearn reference,
which puts the initial gap $f(\mathbf{w}_{0})-f^{*}\approx 0.43$ --- the
$y$-intercept of Figure~\ref{fig:convergence-iter}.

\paragraph{IRLS: linear rate (Theorem~\ref{thm:irls_convergence}).}
The expected behaviour from Chapter~\ref{chap:irls} is linear convergence of the MM iteration.
Figure~\ref{fig:convergence-iter} (left) confirms it: after a fast
transient the gap decreases at a constant per-iteration factor
$\approx 0.848$ in the asymptotic region, reaching $1.5\cdot 10^{-6}$ in
$100$ iterations (iterate change $5.6\cdot 10^{-6}$). The late flattening
toward $\sim\!10^{-6}$ is the accuracy floor of the smoothed surrogate at
$\varepsilon_\text{thr}=10^{-8}$ (Section~\ref{sec:params}), not a
breakdown of the rate.

\paragraph{SGPTL: sublinear rate (Theorem~\ref{thm:dsm_convergence}).}
The expected behaviour from Chapter~\ref{chap:dsm} is an $O(1/\sqrt{i})$
record gap and a non-monotone current value. Figure~\ref{fig:convergence-iter} (right,
$8000$ iterations, $\beta=1$, $\gamma_{\min}=0.05$,
$\delta_{0}=0.1\,f(\mathbf{w}_{0})$, $\rho=0.7$) confirms both: the
record $\bar f^{i}-f^{*}$ follows the sublinear envelope down to
$4.8\cdot 10^{-5}$, and each decade of accuracy costs a $\sim\!100\times$
increase in iterations, exactly the $\varepsilon\mapsto\varepsilon/10
\Rightarrow i\mapsto 100\,i$ scaling of the $O(L^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})$
bound. The current value oscillates around the record, as the theory
allows (the bound controls the record, not the raw iterate).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/convergence_vs_iter.pdf}
    \caption{Optimality gap versus iteration on $H=100$, $M=300$,
    $\lambda_\text{LASSO}=0.1$. Left: IRLS, semilog axis; the annotation
    is the empirical per-iteration factor in the linear region. Right:
    SGPTL, log--log axes, current value (orange, subsampled
    log-uniformly) over the record (red). The record traces the
    $O(1/\sqrt{i})$ envelope of
    Theorem~\ref{thm:dsm_convergence}.}
    \label{fig:convergence-iter}
\end{figure}

Against wall-clock time (Figure~\ref{fig:convergence-time}) SGPTL's
cheaper iteration ($O(MH)$ vs $O(H^{3})$) does not offset the slower
rate at this size: IRLS reaches $10^{-6}$ in $\sim\!3$~ms, SGPTL drops
below $10^{-2}$ within $\sim\!10$~ms and then crawls to $\sim\!5\cdot
10^{-5}$ over $\sim\!150$~ms.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{images/convergence_vs_time.pdf}
    \caption{Optimality gap versus CPU time on the instance of
    Figure~\ref{fig:convergence-iter}, log--log axes.}
    \label{fig:convergence-time}
\end{figure}

Both convergence curves above track the SGPTL \emph{record}
$\bar f^{\,i} = \min_{h\le i} f(\mathbf{w}_{h})$ rather than the raw
current value $f(\mathbf{w}_{i})$, because --- as expected from
Section~\ref{sec:dsm_target_level} --- the subgradient dynamics are not
monotone, and only the record carries the rate guarantee of
Theorem~\ref{thm:dsm_convergence}. Figure~\ref{fig:dsm-nonmonotone} makes
this explicit: the current gap spikes well above the record at several
points along the run, while the record is monotone by construction. This
is also the practical reason the algorithm tracks and returns
$\mathbf{w}_\text{best}$ rather than the last iterate.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/dsm_nonmonotone.pdf}
    \caption{SGPTL on the convergence instance, first $2000$ iterations,
    semilog $y$. The current gap (orange) overshoots the record (black)
    repeatedly; the largest overshoot is annotated.}
    \label{fig:dsm-nonmonotone}
\end{figure}

\section{Cold versus warm start}
\label{sec:warm_cold}

\paragraph{Setting.}
We run \emph{both} algorithms from \emph{both}
initialisations on all three instances: the synthetic
benchmark ($H=100$, $M=300$, $\lambda_\text{LASSO}=0.1$) and the two
ELM-transformed real datasets ($H=200$). Both Theorem~\ref{thm:irls_convergence}
(IRLS) and Theorem~\ref{thm:dsm_convergence} (SGPTL) admit any
$\mathbf{w}_{0}$; what changes between the starts is the initial distance
$\norm{\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}}$, which enters their convergence
bounds multiplicatively. Table~\ref{tab:warm-cold-summary} collects the
final gaps; the rest of the section interprets them.

\paragraph{Order-of-magnitude check on the SGPTL real-data gaps.}
The theorem reads
$\bar f^{k}-f^{*}\le L\norm{\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}}/(\gamma_{\min}\sqrt{k+1})$.
Collapsing the numerator $L\norm{\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}}$ into the
initial objective gap $g_{0}=f(\mathbf{w}_{0})-f^{*}$ (same scaling)
gives the rule of thumb $\bar f^{k}-f^{*}\lesssim g_{0}/(\gamma_{\min}\sqrt{k})$.
With cold start and standardised targets, $g_{0}\approx M_\text{train}/2$
and $\gamma_{\min}=0.05$, so at $k=8\,000$ the envelope predicts
$\sim\!5.6$ on the synthetic, $\sim\!28$ on \texttt{diabetes},
$\sim\!1\,320$ on \texttt{california}. The observed gaps ($2.4\cdot 10^{-5}$,
$1.74$, $\sim\!64$) sit one to five orders of magnitude below it. The
envelope is loose because the worst-case $L$ and sublevel-set radius
overestimate the actual quantities entering the bound on a
well-conditioned design with normalised columns, and the deflection
floor itself binds only on degenerate steps
(Section~\ref{sec:dsm_convergence_conditions}). On the synthetic the
margin is widest; on \texttt{california} it shrinks because the
sublevel-set radius is largest there.

\paragraph{IRLS.}
Both starts converge to $f^{*}$ on every instance; the IRLS curves in
Figure~\ref{fig:irls-warm-cold} flatten to the
$\varepsilon_\text{stop}/\varepsilon_\text{thr}$ floor regardless of
start, so the differentiator is the \emph{iteration count}: cold needs
$\sim\!25\%$ more iterations than warm on \texttt{diabetes} ($1\,118$ vs
$894$) and $\sim\!13\%$ more on \texttt{california} ($115$ vs $102$);
on the synthetic the two coincide within rounding. The pattern matches
the linear rate of Theorem~\ref{thm:irls_convergence}, in which
$\norm{\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}}$ enters multiplicatively.

\paragraph{SGPTL.}
The sublinear rate makes the warm--cold contrast much less decisive
than for IRLS: the bound~\eqref{eq:def-rate-deflected} shrinks the
residual as $1/\sqrt{i}$, so unless $\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{OLS}}$ is
already very close to $\mathbf{w}^{*}$ the two starts end the budget at
comparable gaps. On the synthetic instance they finish within a factor
of two (Figure~\ref{fig:warm-vs-cold}: cold $2.4\cdot 10^{-5}$ with $26$
$\delta$-contractions, warm $4.8\cdot 10^{-5}$ with $22$). On
\texttt{diabetes}-ELM the cold trajectory dips below the warm one past
$\sim\!10^{2}$ iterations and stays there ($1.74$ vs $2.62$; contraction
counts $23$ vs $25$, so the difference is not driven by the patience
schedule).

\texttt{California}-ELM is the qualitative outlier and the source of
the warm-start caveat referenced throughout this report.
$f(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{OLS}})$ sits within $0.46$ of $f^{*}$ before
SGPTL takes its first step, and the Polyak stepsize
$\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}(f(\mathbf{w}_{i})-\text{target})/\norm{\mathbf{d}_{i}}_{2}^{2}$
shrinks with the current gap, so the warm-started iterate barely moves
over $8\,000$ iterations; the warm-start number $0.46$ measures how
close OLS already is to $f^{*}$ on this instance, not the residual SGPTL
reduced from it. The cold start, starting $\sim\!5\,900$ above $f^{*}$,
closes two decades of gap (down to $\sim\!64$) over the same budget.
Figure~\ref{fig:real-data} (Section~\ref{sec:real_data}) overlays both
SGPTL starts.

\paragraph{Defaults.}
For IRLS we use the OLS warm start everywhere in this report. The OLS
factorisation costs $O(MH^{2}+H^{3})$ in floating-point operations and,
measured directly, $0.13$\,ms on \texttt{diabetes}~($H=200$) and $2.9$\,ms
on \texttt{california}~($H=200$), against an IRLS body of $165$\,ms and
$1577$\,ms respectively (\texttt{warm\_start\_cost.csv}); the warm-start
share is below $0.2\%$ of the total in both regimes, so the
$\sim\!13$--$25\%$ iteration savings reported above more than amortise
it. For SGPTL we adopt the cold start $\mathbf{w}_{0}=\mathbf{0}$ as the
default (it is the method's natural initialisation) and pass an explicit
warm start only where its final gap is empirically smaller, as on
\texttt{california} in Table~\ref{tab:real_data}.

\subsection{SGPTL beyond the submitted iteration budget}
\label{sec:dsm_long_run}
The estimate above predicts the SGPTL real-data gaps at $k=8\,000$ from
the rule of thumb $g_{0}/\sqrt{k}$. We push the experiment much further
to see how the gap evolves: SGPTL cold to $i_{\max}=10^{7}$ on
\texttt{diabetes}-ELM ($3.3$\,min wall-clock) and to $i_{\max}=10^{6}$ on
\texttt{california}-ELM ($23$\,min). Figure~\ref{fig:sgptl-long-run}
overlays the empirical record gap against the heuristic envelope
$g_{0}/\sqrt{i}$.

The empirical trace stays \emph{below} the envelope and descends faster
than its worst-case slope (Table~\ref{tab:sgptl-long-run-samples}). On
\texttt{diabetes} the gap drops from $0.26$ at $k=8\,000$ to
$\le 10^{-12}$ by $k=8\cdot 10^{6}$ --- SGPTL reaches the
floating-point floor on this instance with enough budget. On
\texttt{california} it descends from $41$ at $k=8\,000$ to $1.2\cdot
10^{-6}$ at $k=8\cdot 10^{5}$.

\section{Hyperparameter tuning and technique choices}
\label{sec:params}

This section reports sensitivity sweeps on each algorithm separately:
the IRLS parameters $\varepsilon_\text{thr}$ and $\lambda_\text{LASSO}$
and the choice of inner solver (Cholesky vs Jacobi-preconditioned CG)
below, the SGPTL parameters $\gamma_{\min}$, $\delta_{0}$ and $\rho$
after.

\subsection{IRLS: \texorpdfstring{$\varepsilon_\text{thr}$}{eps\_thr}
and \texorpdfstring{$\lambda_\text{LASSO}$}{lambda\_LASSO}}

We sweep both at $H=100$, $M=400$, $100$ iterations
(Figure~\ref{fig:params-irls}). For
$\varepsilon_\text{thr}\in\{10^{-4},\dots,10^{-12}\}$ the final gap
tracks $\varepsilon_\text{thr}$ ($1.4\cdot 10^{-6}$, $1.5\cdot 10^{-8}$,
$5.3\cdot 10^{-10}$ at $10^{-6},10^{-8},10^{-10}$); $10^{-4}$ is too loose
(no sparsity) and $10^{-12}$ hits floating-point limits, so the
$10^{-8}$--$10^{-10}$ range is the default. Sweeping
$\lambda_\text{LASSO}\in\{0.01,\dots,1.0\}$ leaves the convergence speed
roughly invariant ($\lambda$ enters $\mathbf{Q}_{k}$ as a bounded
conditioning factor) while sparsity moves monotonically from $11\%$ to
$95\%$, as the optimality condition~\eqref{eq:optimality} implies.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/params_irls.pdf}
    \caption{IRLS sensitivity. Left: $\varepsilon_\text{thr}$ at
    $\lambda_\text{LASSO}=0.1$. Right: $\lambda_\text{LASSO}$ at
    $\varepsilon_\text{thr}=10^{-8}$.}
    \label{fig:params-irls}
\end{figure}

\subsection{IRLS: inner solver (Cholesky vs.\ CG)}
\label{sec:solver_comparison}
Chapter~\ref{chap:irls} leaves the inner solver of
$\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k+1}=\mathbf{b}$ open between Cholesky
($O(H^{3}/3)$) and CG ($O(pH^{2})$ per step).
Table~\ref{tab:solver_comparison} compares them on $H=100$, $M=400$:
both reach the stopping criterion at iteration $265$ with identical
trajectories, but exact Cholesky takes $11.7$~ms against
$24.4$~ms for Jacobi-preconditioned CG. As IRLS drives inactive weights
to the cap $1/\varepsilon_\text{thr}=10^{8}$, $\kappa(\mathbf{Q}_{k})$
blows up; the Jacobi preconditioner on $\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{Q}_{k})$ is what
keeps CG matching Cholesky iteration-by-iteration, but the iterative
overhead still doubles the wall-clock time. In the project regime
($M\gg H$, $H\le 1000$) Cholesky is the default --- faster, MM-descent
guaranteed unconditionally, no inner tolerance to tune; CG remains a
drop-in for $H$ large enough that the $O(H^{3})$ term dominates.

\subsection{SGPTL: the deflection floor \texorpdfstring{$\gamma_{\min}$}{gamma\_min}}
Theorem~\ref{thm:dsm_convergence} requires $\gamma_{i}\ge\gamma_{\min}>0$
to keep the stepsize clip $\beta_{i}=\min(\beta,\gamma_{i})$ uniformly
bounded away from zero
(Section~\ref{sec:dsm_convergence_conditions}); the greedy
formula~\eqref{eq:gamma_star} of
Section~\ref{sec:dsm_optimal_gamma} can otherwise drive $\gamma_{i}$
arbitrarily close to zero near stationarity. We run two sweeps below to
(i) check whether the floor is needed in practice on ELM~LASSO and
(ii) calibrate its value.

\paragraph{Does the floor matter? (binary test, $\gamma_{\min}\in\{0,\,0.05\}$).}
We compare the algorithm with no floor ($\gamma_{\min}=0$, the raw
greedy) against the floored variant ($\gamma_{\min}=0.05$) on a $3\times
5$ grid: three sizes $(H,M)$ equal to $(50,150)$, $(100,300)$ and
$(200,600)$, five seeds each. The same pattern emerges across the $30$
runs (Figure~\ref{fig:gamma-floor-test}; aggregate over the medium-size
block):

        $\gamma_{\min}$ & final gap & $\Pr\!\{\gamma_{i}\le 0.01\}$ & $\Pr\!\{\text{skip fires}\}$ & $\delta$-contr. \\
        \midrule
        $0.00$ & $4.4\cdot 10^{-2}$ & $99\%$ & $98\%$ & ${\sim}\,2$ \\
        $0.05$ & $1.2\cdot 10^{-4}$ & $0\%$  & $0\%$  & ${\sim}\,19$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The mechanism behind the failure mode of $\gamma_{\min}=0$ is a
chain reaction visible on the figure's leftmost panel. Near stationarity
the greedy $\gamma^{*}$ from~\eqref{eq:gamma_star} approaches zero
because consecutive subgradients align and the numerator
$\norm{\mathbf{d}_{i-1}}^{2}-\inner{\mathbf{g}_{i}}{\mathbf{d}_{i-1}}$
vanishes. With no floor, $\gamma_{i}$ collapses below $10^{-10}$ from
roughly iteration~$50$ onward and stays there for the rest of the run.
The clip $\beta_{i}=\min(1,\gamma_{i})$ collapses with it, the Polyak
step~\eqref{eq:sr_step} becomes positive but minuscule, and once $f_{i}$
no longer beats the target the numerator-skip safeguard fires on $98\%$
of iterations: the
iterate freezes and $\delta$ contracts only $\sim\!2$ times instead of
$\sim\!19$, leaving the gap stuck at $\sim\!4\cdot 10^{-2}$ (centre and
right panels). With $\gamma_{\min}=0.05$ the floor binds whenever the
greedy would hit zero, $\beta_{i}$ stays $\ge 0.05$, the Polyak step
remains effective, and the staircase descent of $\delta$ that the theory
predicts takes place --- the gap drops by two more orders of magnitude.
The chain reaction is structural to ELM~LASSO with the greedy $\gamma$
(the failure depends on the algorithm's choice of $\gamma$, not on the
specific instance), and we observed it on all $15$ unfloored seeds.

\paragraph{Which value of $\gamma_{\min}$ (calibration sweep).}
We sweep $\gamma_{\min}$ over $0.05$, $0.1$, $0.2$ and $0.5$ on the
convergence instance. The final gap lies in $[10^{-7},10^{-5}]$ across
the four values, with $\gamma_{\min}=0.05$ marginally best. Larger
values force the clip to bind on iterations where the greedy
$\gamma_{i}$ would otherwise lie strictly inside $(\gamma_{\min},1]$,
so we adopt $\gamma_{\min}=0.05$.

\subsection{SGPTL: \texorpdfstring{$\delta_{0}$}{delta\_0} and
\texorpdfstring{$\rho$}{rho}}
Of the four target-level parameters of SGPTL, $\beta$ and $R$ are
settled analytically in Section~\ref{sec:dsm_parameters} and not swept
here. The remaining two, $\delta_{0}=c\,f(\mathbf{w}_{0})$ and $\rho$,
have intrinsically empirical tradeoffs and are calibrated below. The sweeps of
Figure~\ref{fig:params-dsm} ($H=100$, $M=400$, $5000$ iterations) show
the method is robust to both: $c\in\{0.01,\dots,1.0\}$ at $\rho=0.7$
gives final gaps in $[5.6\cdot 10^{-5},1.6\cdot 10^{-4}]$, within one
order of magnitude across two decades of $\delta_{0}$;
$\rho\in\{0.3,\dots,0.95\}$ at $c=0.1$ gives
$1.98\cdot 10^{-5}$ up to $8.12\cdot 10^{-4}$ with $7$ to $91$
contractions. Small $\rho$ wins on this instance, but a smoke test over
the synthetic and the two ELM matrices places the per-instance best at
$\rho=0.3,0.3,0.7$; only $\rho\in[0.3,0.7]$ is within a factor of two of
the best everywhere, and $\rho\ge 0.9$ loses by one to two orders of
magnitude. We adopt $\rho=0.7$ and $c=0.1$ as robust defaults.

\subsection{SGPTL: \texorpdfstring{$\delta_{0}$}{delta\_0} against the

\label{sec:delta0_oracle}
The operational choice is $\delta_{0}=c\,f(\mathbf{w}_{0})$, settled in
Section~\ref{sec:dsm_parameters} and used in every run of this chapter.
The comparison below is purely an abstract sensitivity study: we ask, as
a sanity check, how the admissible scale would compare against the
hypothetical $\delta_{0}=c\,f^{*}$ \emph{if} $f^{*}$ were known. Tuning
algorithmic parameters against $f^{*}$ is not allowed in practice (it
would amount to using the optimum to set the optimizer, the
optimisation analogue of training on the test set), so the
$f^{*}$-based scale enters only as a reference point in this and the
next subsection and is never used to choose the default.

We run both scales on five instances over $c\in\{0.01,\dots,1\}$. The

regime where SGPTL is in fact optimising (Table~\ref{tab:delta0-ratio}).
The one exception is \texttt{diabetes}~$H=50$ at $c\ge 0.5$, where an
over-aggressive $\delta_{0}$ overshoots the sufficient-descent test
from the first iteration and both scales stall at a common plateau (a
regime limit of the sweep, not a finding about the scales). The full
per-instance grid is in Appendix~\ref{app:delta0-families}.

\section{IRLS versus SGPTL}
\label{sec:comparison_results}
Chapter~\ref{chap:comparison} contrasts the linear cubic-per-iteration
IRLS with the sublinear cheap-per-iteration SGPTL and predicts IRLS
dominance for moderate-to-high accuracy in the $M\gg H$ regime. The
following subsections test this prediction on solution quality and
sparsity, scaling, and iterations to a target.

\subsection{Solution quality and sparsity}
\label{sec:quality}
On $H=50$, $M=200$, $\lambda_\text{LASSO}=0.1$ (reference sparsity
$88\%$), IRLS reaches $f-f^{*}\le 10^{-6}$ in $101$ iterations at
$\|\mathbf{w}_\text{IRLS}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\|_{2}=2.95\cdot 10^{-6}$; SGPTL
reaches $1.0\cdot 10^{-4}$, $\sim\!35\times$ looser. The two need
\emph{different} sparsity thresholds, as Section~\ref{sec:dsm_properties}
predicts: IRLS pins inactive weights near $\varepsilon_\text{thr}=10^{-8}$,
whereas SGPTL's inactive components sit at the residual-rate scale
$L/(\gamma_{\min}\sqrt{i})\in[10^{-4},10^{-3}]$. Applying the
\emph{same} hard threshold to both (Table~\ref{tab:support_recovery}):
at $10^{-6}$ SGPTL's $F_{1}$ collapses to $0.36$ because its inactive
coefficients sit above the cutoff; at $10^{-3}$, above the residual
floor, both recover the support exactly.

\subsection{Scalability}
\label{sec:scalability}

\paragraph{Setting.}
We grow the ELM size $H\in\{50,100,500,1000,2000\}$ at $M=5H$ on the
synthetic instance and time both algorithms at a \emph{fixed iteration
budget}: $100$ Cholesky iterations for IRLS, $3000$ subgradient
iterations for SGPTL. The per-iteration costs $O(MH^{2}+H^{3})$ for IRLS
and $O(MH)$ for SGPTL are derived in
Chapter~\ref{chap:comparison} (Table~\ref{tab:comp_cost}) and
appear as the $O(H^{3})$ and $O(H^{2})$ slopes drawn in
Figure~\ref{fig:scalability} (left) at $M=5H$.

\paragraph{What we measure.}
Both algorithms get more expensive as $H$ grows
(Table~\ref{tab:scalability}), but IRLS stays faster than SGPTL
throughout the tested range and reaches a final gap roughly two
orders of magnitude smaller. At $H=2000$: IRLS takes $2.4$\,s to reach
gap $10^{-5}$; SGPTL takes $30$\,s to reach $5.3\cdot 10^{-4}$. The
right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:scalability} confirms the per-iteration
ordering --- SGPTL is cheaper there --- but the cumulative time tells
the opposite story because SGPTL needs many more iterations.

\paragraph{Why the fixed-budget table flatters SGPTL.}
This comparison fixes iterations, not accuracy. To reach a target
$\varepsilon$ Theorem~\ref{thm:dsm_convergence} forces
$\Omega(L^{2}/(\gamma_{\min}^{2}\varepsilon^{2}))$ SGPTL iterations with
$L$ growing in $H$; the cost-to-accuracy ratio is then much wider than
Table~\ref{tab:scalability} suggests, as Section~\ref{sec:iters-to-eps}
makes quantitative.

\section{Validation on real datasets}
\label{sec:real_data}
We need an independent $f^{*}$ to report gaps on these two instances. The
natural off-the-shelf candidate is sklearn's coordinate-descent
\texttt{Lasso}, but on the ELM-transformed matrices sklearn never reaches
its own \texttt{tol} stop: at $\texttt{max\_iter}=10^{5}$,
$\texttt{tol}=10^{-10}$, it returns at the iteration cap with a final
duality gap two to six orders of magnitude above tolerance, and a
residual gap to $f^{*}$ of $+1.4\cdot 10^{-3}$ on \texttt{diabetes} and
$+2.82$ on \texttt{california}.
sklearn CD is therefore not a usable oracle on these problems, and we
build $f^{*}$ instead by running IRLS to convergence
($k_{\max}=500,\varepsilon_\text{stop}=10^{-14}$) and cross-validating it
against CVXPY's interior-point solver Clarabel. The two are structurally
different (MM~+~linear solve vs.\ primal--dual interior point) and agree
to ten significant digits on both instances.

Both datasets use $H=200$ sigmoid units, $\lambda_\text{LASSO}=0.1$.
The comparison criterion is fixed-accuracy rather than fixed-iteration:
each method runs until $f - f^{*} \le 10^{-6}$, and the cost is
reported in iterations and wall time
(Table~\ref{tab:real_data}). IRLS runs with tight smoothing
$\varepsilon_\text{thr}=10^{-12}$ from the OLS warm start; CVXPY-Clarabel
runs at \texttt{tol\_gap}$=10^{-9}$; SGPTL runs cold from
$\mathbf{w}_{0}=\mathbf{0}$ with $\beta=1$, $\gamma_{\min}=0.05$, $R=1$,

counts and wall-times in Table~\ref{tab:real_data} are measured directly:
we rerun SGPTL with $i_{\max}$ set just above the long-run crossing
($3.6\cdot 10^{5}$ on \texttt{diabetes}, $1.03\cdot 10^{6}$ on
\texttt{california}) and read the wall-clock at the first iteration where
the record gap falls below $10^{-6}$

$i_{\max}=10^{7}$/$10^{6}$ is the one of Section~\ref{sec:dsm_long_run}.

\section{Initial design and corrections}
\label{sec:initial_design}
Two SGPTL design choices in Algorithm~\ref{alg:dsm} were reached only
after correcting an earlier implementation. We document each as
\emph{before}~/~\emph{why~wrong}~/~\emph{after}~/~\emph{why~right}, then
compare the numbers the two implementations produce on the convergence
instance.

\paragraph{Target-level scale $\delta_{0}$.}
\emph{Before:} $\delta_{0}=c\,f^{*}$.
\emph{Why wrong:} $f^{*}$ is the very quantity SGPTL exists to estimate,
so using it to set an algorithmic parameter assumes access to the
answer; it is the optimisation analogue of training on the test set
and disqualifies the result.
\emph{After:} $\delta_{0}=c\,f(\mathbf{w}_{0})$.
\emph{Why right:} $f(\mathbf{w}_{0})$ is computable from the starting
point alone; the sweep in Section~\ref{sec:delta0_oracle} confirms it
tracks the oracle scale within a factor of two on every test instance,
so the admissible choice loses nothing in practice.

\paragraph{Contraction trigger and the radius $R$.}
\emph{Before:} $R=10\sqrt{i_{\max}}\!\approx\!894$ together with an
iteration-count fallback that contracted $\delta$ every
$\lceil i_{\max}/30\rceil$ steps.
\emph{Why wrong:} the realistic per-iteration step length on this
problem is $\alpha_{i}\norm{\mathbf{d}_{i}}\sim 10^{-3}$, so cumulative
travel never reaches $R\!\approx\!894$ and the travel-distance branch
never fires; \emph{every} contraction is driven by the iteration
counter, which is inconsistent
with~\cite[Lemma~3.8]{dantonio2009deflected}'s requirement that each
contraction follow $r_{k}>R$ travel.
\emph{After:} $R=1$ matched to the realistic step length, fallback
removed.
\emph{Why right:} contractions now fire on travel as the lemma
requires, so the $\delta$ schedule reflects the actual descent.

\paragraph{Before vs.\ after on the convergence instance.}
Table~\ref{tab:before-after-numbers} compares the two implementations
on the same instance ($H=100$, $M=300$, $\lambda_\text{LASSO}=0.1$,
$i_{\max}=8000$). The pre-correction contraction count is $\sim\!95$
(all from the fallback, none from travel-distance); post-correction it
is $\sim\!22$ (all from travel-distance). The record gap drops by three
orders of magnitude under the old setup, but this is not convergence:
it is the $\delta$ schedule collapsing on a counter rather than on the
optimisation state, decoupling target levels from actual progress. The
post-correction record gap $4.8\cdot 10^{-5}$ is the figure the theory
of Theorem~\ref{thm:dsm_convergence} bounds for SGPTL on this instance.
The qualitative ranking against IRLS (Chapter~\ref{chap:comparison}) is
unaffected.
