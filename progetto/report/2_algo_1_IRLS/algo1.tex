\chapter{Algorithm 1: Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares}
\label{chap:irls}

Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) is a classical algorithm for solving
optimization problems involving non-smooth objective functions of the form of a $p$-norm.
Its core idea is to handle non-smoothness
by solving a sequence of \textit{weighted} least-squares problems, each of which is
smooth and admits an efficient closed-form solution. In our setting, IRLS is used
to minimize the $L_1$-regularized least-squares objective:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^H} f(\mathbf{w})
    = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{Xw} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2}_{g(\mathbf{w})}
    + \underbrace{\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|_1}_{h(\mathbf{w})}
    \label{eq:irls_objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times H}$, $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^M$,
and $\lambda > 0$ is the regularization hyperparameter.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The core idea: approximating the \texorpdfstring{$L_1$}{L1} norm}
\label{sec:irls_idea}

The difficulty with problem~\eqref{eq:irls_objective} is the $L_1$ penalty term
$h(\mathbf{w}) = \lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|_1 = \lambda\sum_{i=1}^H |w_i|$: it is convex,
but has sharp corners (\textit{kinks}) wherever any $w_i = 0$, making it
non-differentiable in those points. Classical gradient-based algorithms cannot be
applied directly.

The key observation is the following identity \cite{daubechies2008iterativelyreweightedsquaresminimization}: for any $(w_k)_i \neq 0$,
\begin{equation}
    |w_i| = \frac{w_i^2}{|w_i|} \approx \frac{w_i^2}{|(w_k)_i|}
    \label{eq:irls_approx}
\end{equation}
where $(w_k)_i$ is the value of the $i$-th component at the current iteration $k$.
This is a \textit{quadratic approximation} of $|w_i|$ in the variable $w_i$, where
the denominator $|(w_k)_i|$ is treated as a fixed constant.
Summing over all components, we obtain:
\begin{equation}
    \|\mathbf{w}\|_1 = \sum_{i=1}^H |w_i| \approx \sum_{i=1}^H \frac{w_i^2}{|(w_k)_i|}
    = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{W}_k^T\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{w}
    \label{eq:irls_quadratic}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times H}$ is a diagonal matrix with entries:
\begin{equation}
    (\mathbf{W}_k)_{ii} = |(w_{k})_i|^{-1/2}
    \label{eq:irls_weights_exact}
\end{equation}
This turns the non-smooth $L_1$ term into a \textit{smooth, quadratic} expression
$\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{W}_k^T \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{w}$. Intuitively, $\mathbf{W}_k^T\mathbf{W}_k$ applies
\textit{personalized penalization} to each component of $\mathbf{w}$: components with
small magnitude at iteration $k$ receive a large weight (strong penalization, pushing
them toward zero), while components with large magnitude receive a small weight
(weaker penalization, leaving them relatively free).

\paragraph{Handling near-zero components.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:irls_weights_exact} becomes numerically unstable when $(w_k)_i
\approx 0$, since $|(w_k)_i|^{-1/2}$ can blow up. To prevent division by (near) zero,
a \textbf{safety threshold} $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}} > 0$ is introduced:
\begin{equation}
    (\mathbf{W}_k)_{ii} = \max\!\bigl(|(w_k)_i|,\, \varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}}\bigr)^{-1/2}
    \label{eq:irls_weights}
\end{equation}
Typical values are $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}} \in [10^{-6}, 10^{-10}]$.
Setting $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}}$ too large degrades the approximation of $|w_i|$
and may prevent convergence to a truly sparse solution; setting it too small risks
numerical instability. This parameter is an important implementation choice.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The weighted least-squares subproblem}
\label{sec:irls_subproblem}

At each iteration $k$, substituting the quadratic approximation~\eqref{eq:irls_quadratic}
into the objective~\eqref{eq:irls_objective} gives the following subproblem:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{w}_{k+1} = \underset{\mathbf{w}}{\arg\min}
    \;\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{Xw} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{w}\|^2_2
    \label{eq:irls_subproblem}
\end{equation}
This is a standard \textbf{weighted least-squares problem with $L_2$ regularization}
(Ridge regression), which is smooth and strictly convex. Its closed-form solution
follows from setting the gradient to zero.

\begin{proposition}[Weighted normal equations]
    \label{prop:irls_normal}
    The unique solution of~\eqref{eq:irls_subproblem} satisfies:
    \begin{equation}
        \bigl(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + 2\lambda\mathbf{W}_k^T \mathbf{W}_k \bigr)\,
        \mathbf{w}_{k+1} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}
        \label{eq:irls_normal}
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    The gradient of the objective in~\eqref{eq:irls_subproblem} is:
    \[
        \nabla f_k(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{Xw} - \mathbf{y})
        + 2\lambda\mathbf{W}_k^T \mathbf{W}_k\mathbf{w}
    \]
    Setting this to zero yields~\eqref{eq:irls_normal}.
    The coefficient matrix $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + 2\lambda\mathbf{W}_k^T\mathbf{W}_k$
    is symmetric positive definite (it is the sum of $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} \succeq 0$
    and $2\lambda\mathbf{W}_k^T\mathbf{W}_k \succ 0$ since $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}} > 0$),
    so the system has a unique solution.
\end{proof}

Since $\mathbf{W}_k$ is diagonal, defining $\mathbf{Q}_k = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}
+2\lambda\mathbf{W}_k^T\mathbf{W}_k$ and $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$,
equation~\eqref{eq:irls_normal} reduces to:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{Q}_k\, \mathbf{w}_{k+1} = \mathbf{b}
    \label{eq:irls_linear_system}
\end{equation}
This is an $H \times H$ symmetric positive definite (SPD) linear system, which can
be solved efficiently at each iteration. Note that $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$
can be pre-computed once before the iterative loop, since it does not depend on $k$.
Similarly, $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is pre-computed once, and only the
diagonal $2\lambda\mathbf{W}_k^T\mathbf{W}_k$ is updated at each iteration.

\paragraph{Solving the linear system.}
Because $\mathbf{Q}_k$ is SPD, two efficient methods can be used:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cholesky factorization:}~\cite{trefethen1997numerical} computes $\mathbf{Q}_k = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T$
    in $O(H^3/3)$ operations, then solves two triangular systems in $O(H^2)$. This
    is the direct method of choice for moderate-sized problems.
    \item \textbf{Conjugate Gradient (CG):}~\cite{trefethen1997numerical} an iterative method that converges in at
    most $H$ steps in exact arithmetic, with convergence rate depending on the condition
    number $\kappa(\mathbf{Q}_k)$. Preferable for large-scale problems where $H$ is
    too large for Cholesky.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Majorization-Minimization interpretation}
\label{sec:irls_mm}

IRLS can be understood within the broader framework of
\textbf{Majorization-Minimization (MM) algorithms} \cite{nguyen2016introductionmmalgorithmsmachine}. At each iteration, rather than
minimizing the original objective $f(\mathbf{w})$ directly, IRLS builds a
\textit{surrogate function} $Q(\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{w}_k)$ that:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Majorizes} $f$: $Q(\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{w}_k) \geq f(\mathbf{w})$
    for all $\mathbf{w}$;
    \item \textbf{Touches} $f$ at the current iterate: $Q(\mathbf{w}_k, \mathbf{w}_k)
    = f(\mathbf{w}_k)$;
    \item Is \textbf{easy to minimize}: $Q(\cdot, \mathbf{w}_k)$ is a smooth quadratic.
\end{enumerate}
In our case, the surrogate is:
\begin{equation}
    Q(\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{w}_k) = \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{Xw} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2
    + \lambda\|\mathbf{W}_k\mathbf{w}\|_2^2
\end{equation}
The MM construction guarantees a key property: since minimizing $Q(\cdot, \mathbf{w}_k)$
gives $\mathbf{w}_{k+1}$, we have $Q(\mathbf{w}_{k+1}, \mathbf{w}_k) \leq
Q(\mathbf{w}_k, \mathbf{w}_k) = f(\mathbf{w}_k)$, and since
$f(\mathbf{w}_{k+1}) \leq Q(\mathbf{w}_{k+1}, \mathbf{w}_k)$, it follows that:
\begin{equation}
    f(\mathbf{w}_{k+1}) \leq f(\mathbf{w}_k)
\end{equation}
That is, \textbf{IRLS monotonically decreases the objective function} at every
iteration. This is a much stronger guarantee than what the Deflected Subgradient
Method provides (see Chapter~\ref{chap:dsm}).

\section{The complete algorithm}
\label{sec:irls_algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
    \caption{Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) for LASSO}
    \label{alg:irls}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times H}$, $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^M$,
        $\lambda > 0$, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}} > 0$, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{stop}} > 0$,
        $k_{\max}$
        \State Pre-compute $\mathbf{A} \leftarrow \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$,
        $\mathbf{b} \leftarrow \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$
        \State Initialize $\mathbf{w}_0 \leftarrow \mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}$
        (ordinary least-squares solution)
        \For{$k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, k_{\max}$}
            \State Compute weights: $(\mathbf{W}_k)_{ii} \leftarrow
            \max(|(w_k)_i|,\, \varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}})^{-1/2}$ for all $i$
            \State Assemble system: $\mathbf{Q}_k \leftarrow \mathbf{A}
            + 2\lambda\mathbf{W}_k^T\mathbf{W}_k$
            \State Solve: $\mathbf{Q}_k\,\mathbf{w}_{k+1} = \mathbf{b}$
            \quad \textit{(via Cholesky or CG)}
            \If{$\|\mathbf{w}_{k+1} - \mathbf{w}_k\| \,/\, \max(1, \|\mathbf{w}_k\|)
            < \varepsilon_{\mathrm{stop}}$}
                \State \textbf{break} \quad \textit{(convergence reached)}
            \EndIf
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $\mathbf{w}_{k+1}$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Initialization.} The algorithm is initialized with $\mathbf{w}_0 = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}$,
the solution to the unregularized least-squares problem. This provides a reasonable
starting point: the initial weights $\mathbf{w}_0$ reflect the magnitude of the
unregularized solution, giving the algorithm sensible information about which
components are likely to be large.

\paragraph{Stopping criterion.} The algorithm terminates when the relative change
between successive iterates falls below $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{stop}}$:
\[
    \frac{\|\mathbf{w}_{k+1} - \mathbf{w}_k\|}{\max(1, \|\mathbf{w}_k\|)}
    < \varepsilon_{\mathrm{stop}}
\]
The normalization by $\max(1, \|\mathbf{w}_k\|)$ makes the criterion scale-invariant,
avoiding a false trigger when $\|\mathbf{w}_k\|$ is very small.

\section{Convergence analysis}
\label{sec:irls_convergence}

\paragraph{Monotone decrease.}
As derived in Section~\ref{sec:irls_mm} from the MM framework, IRLS
monotonically decreases the objective: $f(\mathbf{w}_{k+1}) \leq f(\mathbf{w}_k)$
for all $k$. Since $f$ is bounded below by zero, the sequence $\{f(\mathbf{w}_k)\}$
converges.

\begin{theorem}[Fixed-point consistency]
    \label{thm:irls_convergence}
    If the sequence $\{\mathbf{w}_k\}$ generated by IRLS converges to a point
    $\mathbf{w}^*$ such that $|(w^*)_i| > \varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}}$ for all $i$,
    then $\mathbf{w}^*$ satisfies the optimality conditions~\eqref{eq:optimality}
    of the original problem~\eqref{eq:irls_objective}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch (adapted from \cite{daubechies2008iterativelyreweightedsquaresminimization})]
    At a fixed point $\mathbf{w}_k = \mathbf{w}_{k+1} = \mathbf{w}^*$, the
    weighted normal equations~\eqref{eq:irls_normal} give:
    \[
        \mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{Xw}^* - \mathbf{y}) + 2\lambda\mathbf{W}_*^T\mathbf{W}_*\mathbf{w}^*
        = 0
    \]
    Since $(\mathbf{W}_*)_{ii} = |w^*_i|^{-1/2}$, we have
    $(\mathbf{W}_*^T\mathbf{W}_*\mathbf{w}^*)_i = |w^*_i|^{-1} w^*_i = \mathrm{sign}(w^*_i)$,
    so this reduces to:
    \[
        \mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{Xw}^* - \mathbf{y}) + 2\lambda\,\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{w}^*)
        = 0
    \]
    which is exactly the first-order optimality condition $0 \in \partial f(\mathbf{w}^*)$
    (equation~\eqref{eq:optimality}) when all components $w^*_i \neq 0$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Convergence rate.}
IRLS converges typically in a \textbf{linear} (geometric) fashion: the error
$\|\mathbf{w}_k - \mathbf{w}^*\|$ decreases approximately by a constant factor at
each iteration. On a semilogarithmic plot of $f(\mathbf{w}_k) - f^*$ versus
iteration number, this manifests as a straight line (linear decrease in log scale).

The convergence rate is governed by the condition number
$\kappa(\mathbf{Q}_k) = \kappa(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + 2\lambda\mathbf{W}_k^T\mathbf{W}_k)$
of the linear systems being solved. The $L_1$ regularization term
$2\lambda\mathbf{W}_k^T\mathbf{W}_k$ acts as a preconditioner that adds a positive
diagonal to $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$, which generally \textit{improves} conditioning
compared to the unregularized system. In practice, IRLS reaches high accuracy in
10--50 iterations.

\paragraph{Verification of hypotheses for the ELM problem.}
We check that the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:irls_convergence} are satisfied
by~\eqref{eq:irls_objective}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Convergence of the sequence.}
Since $f$ is strictly convex (Proposition~\ref{prop:irls_prop}) and IRLS monotonically
decreases $f$ (Section~\ref{sec:irls_mm}), the sequence $\{f(\mathbf{w}_k)\}$ converges.
For the ELM problem, $\mathbf{X} = \sigma(\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{origin}}\mathbf{W}_1)$
has full column rank with high probability (random $\mathbf{W}_1$, nonlinear $\sigma$) \cite{HUANG2006489},
so $f$ is strictly convex and the unique minimiser $\mathbf{w}^*$ exists.
\item \textbf{Non-zero components at convergence.}
The hypothesis $|(w^*)_i| > \varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}}$ for all $i$ is satisfied for
non-zero components of the sparse solution. Components that converge to exactly zero
(within $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}}$) are excluded from the optimality condition,
which is acceptable since they correspond to inactive features.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Computational cost}
\label{sec:irls_cost}

The per-iteration cost of IRLS breaks down as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Weight update} $\mathbf{W}_k$: $O(H)$ — simply iterating over
    the components of $\mathbf{w}_k$.
    \item \textbf{Matrix update} $\mathbf{Q}_k = \mathbf{A} + 2\lambda\mathbf{W}_k^T\mathbf{W}_k$:
    $O(H)$ — since $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is pre-computed and only
    the diagonal changes.
    \item \textbf{Linear system solve} $\mathbf{Q}_k \mathbf{w}_{k+1} = \mathbf{b}$:
    $O(H^3/3)$ with Cholesky, or $O(kH^2)$ with CG for $k$ CG steps.
\end{itemize}
The dominant cost per iteration is the linear system solve: $O(H^3)$ with Cholesky.
However, the total number of IRLS iterations is typically small (10--50), and the
pre-computation of $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ (costing $O(MH^2)$) is
amortized over all iterations. The overall cost is therefore:
\[
    O(MH^2) + k_{\mathrm{IRLS}} \cdot O(H^3)
\]
For problems where $H \ll M$ (many training samples, moderate hidden layer size),
the pre-computation dominates and IRLS is very efficient.

\section{Expected behavior on our problem}
\label{sec:irls_expected}

Based on the theoretical analysis, the following behavior is expected when applying
IRLS to the ELM LASSO problem~\eqref{eq:irls_objective}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Monotone convergence.}
    The objective $f(\mathbf{w}_k)$ decreases at every iteration, making IRLS
    straightforward to monitor and debug. There are no oscillations.
    \item \textbf{Linear convergence rate.}
    On a semilogarithmic plot of $f(\mathbf{w}_k) - f^*$ versus iteration number,
    the curve appears as an approximately straight line. The slope depends on the
    conditioning of $\mathbf{Q}_k$ and on $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}}$.
    \item \textbf{Sparse solutions.}
    As iterations proceed, components $(w_k)_i$ that are small receive increasingly
    large weights, which pushes them further toward zero. At convergence, many
    components are \textit{exactly zero} (or within $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}}$ of
    zero), yielding a genuinely sparse weight vector.
    \item \textbf{Effect of $\lambda$.}
    Larger values of $\lambda$ produce sparser solutions but higher training error.
    Smaller $\lambda$ retains more components but may overfit. The convergence speed
    may also vary with $\lambda$, since it affects the conditioning of $\mathbf{Q}_k$.
    \item \textbf{Sensitivity to $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}}$.}
    A smaller $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}}$ yields a better approximation to the true
    $L_1$ norm and promotes more exact sparsity, but risks numerical instability if
    components approach zero very closely. A value around $10^{-8}$ is typically
    a good compromise.
\end{itemize}
